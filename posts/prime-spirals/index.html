Prime Spirals

pure
number-theory
Plot every prime in polar coordinates and a galaxy appears — spiral arms, dark gaps, broken teeth. Decoding the illusion rediscovers rational approximations of π, Euler’s totient, and Dirichlet’s theorem.
Published

June 10, 2026

“A mathematician, like a painter or a poet, is a maker of patterns. If his patterns are more permanent than theirs, it is because they are made with ideas.”

— G. H. Hardy, A Mathematician’s Apology (1940)

Some of the best mathematics I know starts with a completely pointless act. Here is one: take the prime numbers \(2, 3, 5, 7, 11, \dots\) and plot each prime \(p\) in polar coordinates at the point \((r, \theta) = (p, p)\) — its distance from the origin is \(p\), and its angle, measured in radians, is also \(p\). There is no reason to do this. Radians and radii have nothing to do with primality; we are just feeding the same number into two slots because the slots are there.

And yet, zoom out, and the dots arrange themselves into a galaxy: dozens of spiral arms swirling out from the center, with whole sectors gone dark, as if some arms had been deleted. Zoom out further and the spirals straighten into crisp rays shooting outward — mostly bundled in groups of four, like the teeth of a comb, with the occasional tooth snapped off.

The first time you see this (I met it through Grant Sanderson’s video on the phenomenon [1]), it is hard not to suspect you’ve stumbled onto some secret structure of the primes. The truth is better than that. The pattern is a kind of optical illusion — but decoding the illusion, piece by piece, walks you straight through some of the most beautiful ideas in number theory, ending at a genuine landmark: Dirichlet’s theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions. Let’s take the walk.

The phenomenon

First, look at the thing itself. Here are the primes plotted at \((p, p)\), at three zoom levels.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def primes_upto(n):
    sieve = np.ones(n + 1, dtype=bool); sieve[:2] = False
    for k in range(2, int(n**0.5) + 1):
        if sieve[k]:
            sieve[k*k::k] = False
    return np.flatnonzero(sieve)

p = primes_upto(300_000)
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(9.5, 3.5),
                         subplot_kw={"projection": "polar"})
for ax, lim, s in zip(axes, [3_000, 30_000, 300_000], [3, 0.6, 0.15]):
    q = p[p < lim]
    ax.scatter(q % (2*np.pi), q, s=s, color="#1f4e79", lw=0)
    ax.set_xticks([]); ax.set_yticks([])
    ax.set_title(f"primes up to {lim:,}", fontsize=9)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

Take a moment with the left panel. Count the arms, notice the dark wedges where arms ought to be. Then look at the right panel: straight rays, in tidy clusters of four, and — squint — every so often a ray missing, a broken tooth in the comb.

Two mysteries, then. Why spirals and rays at all? And who is deleting the missing ones?

Mystery one: the spirals are not about primes

Here is the move that cracks the whole thing open, and it’s the oldest trick in the debugging handbook: change one thing at a time. What happens if we drop the primality requirement and plot every whole number at \((n, n)\)?

lim = 3_000
n = np.arange(1, lim)
q = p[p < lim]
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(8, 4),
                         subplot_kw={"projection": "polar"})
for ax, pts, title in [(axes[0], n, "all integers"),
                       (axes[1], q, "primes only")]:
    ax.scatter(pts % (2*np.pi), pts, s=2, color="#1f4e79", lw=0)
    ax.set_xticks([]); ax.set_yticks([]); ax.set_title(title, fontsize=10)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

The spirals are still there — denser, cleaner, with every arm intact. So the swirling structure has nothing to do with primes whatsoever. It is a geometric by-product of how all whole numbers land when you spin them around the origin. The primes are just along for the ride, occupying a subset of the arms. That immediately splits our investigation in two: first understand why integers make spirals, then understand which arms the primes are allowed to ride.

So: why do the integers make spirals? Walk the sequence step by step. Going from \(n\) to \(n+1\) moves you one unit further out and one radian further around. A radian is just a hair less than a sixth of a full turn, because a full turn is \(2\pi \approx 6.283\) radians. So watch what happens after six steps: you have rotated by \(6\) radians — almost, but not quite, a full circle, falling short by about \(0.283\) radians.

Hold that picture. The number \(n + 6\) lands at almost the same angle as \(n\), just one notch further out and rotated back a sliver. And \(n + 12\) repeats the trick, and \(n + 18\), and so on. Each chain of numbers spaced \(6\) apart forms a gently curving arm. How many such chains are there? Exactly six — one for each possible remainder upon dividing by \(6\). The six spiral arms you see near the origin are nothing more than the residue classes mod 6, rendered geometrically. The arms curve because \(6\) is a good-but-not-great approximation of \(2\pi\); the leftover \(0.283\) radians per cycle is the twist.

This kind of pattern shows up all the time in mathematics: a near-miss between two periods creates a slow drift. (It’s the same reason the moon shows us slightly different faces over a month, and the same beat phenomenon you hear when two guitar strings are almost in tune.)

Now, can we do better than “six steps is almost a turn”? Ask the question properly: we want whole numbers \(a\) and \(b\) with \(a\) radians very close to \(b\) full turns — that is, \(\frac{a}{b} \approx 2\pi\). This is the classical problem of rational approximation, and \(\pi\) has two famous star approximations. You might have already guessed the first: \(\pi \approx \frac{22}{7}\). Doubling it, \(\frac{44}{7} \approx 2\pi\), which says that 44 steps make almost exactly 7 full turns, overshooting by a mere \(0.0177\) radians. So at a medium zoom, the integers organize themselves into \(44\) chains — the residue classes mod \(44\) — and because the per-cycle twist is so tiny, these \(44\) arms curve far more gently than the six did. That is the galaxy in the left panel.

And the second star: the stunning approximation \(\pi \approx \frac{355}{113}\), known in China as Zu Chongzhi’s milü, accurate to six decimal places. Doubling: \(\frac{710}{113} \approx 2\pi\), i.e. 710 steps make almost exactly 113 full turns, with an error around \(6 \times 10^{-5}\) radians. At this scale the residual twist is invisible — a curvature so slight your screen cannot render it — and the \(710\) residue classes mod \(710\) appear as 710 dead-straight rays. That is the right panel.

Pause and chew on this, because it’s the heart of mystery one: the spirals-then-rays progression is just the continued fraction expansion of \(2\pi\) made visible. Each zoom level snaps to the best rational approximation available at that scale — \(\frac{6}{1}\), then \(\frac{44}{7}\), then \(\frac{710}{113}\) — and the quality of the approximation controls how straight the arms look. A pointless doodle, and it hands you rational approximation theory unprompted.

Mystery two: the missing arms

Back to the primes, and to the deleted arms. Remember what an arm is: all the numbers with a fixed remainder mod \(6\) (or \(44\), or \(710\)). So asking “which arms survive in the prime picture?” is asking: which remainders can a prime have?

Start small, with mod \(6\). A number with remainder \(0\), \(2\), or \(4\) is even; remainder \(3\) means divisible by \(3\). None of those arms can contain a prime — except the lone primes \(2\) and \(3\) themselves, a single dot each, invisible at any zoom. Primes can only live on the arms with remainder \(1\) or \(5\), the remainders that share no factor with \(6\). Six arms, four deleted, two survive. If you plot primes up to a few hundred you see exactly two spiral arms.

The same sieve explains the galaxy’s dark sectors. We have \(44 = 4 \times 11\). Any arm whose remainder is even is wiped out; so is any arm whose remainder is a multiple of \(11\). Count the survivors: of the \(44\) residue classes, only the \(20\) that are coprime to 44 can host primes. Twenty arms out of forty-four — nearly half the galaxy goes dark, in whole wedges at a time. Go back and count the arms in the first figure’s left panel: twenty.

And now the comb. We have \(710 = 2 \times 5 \times 71\), and each prime factor leaves its own distinctive fingerprint on the picture:

  • The factor 2 deletes every even ray — half the comb gone, leaving \(355\) rays that alternate with gaps. This is why the surviving rays come in evenly spaced pairs of neighbors-but-one.
  • The factor 5 deletes every fifth ray among the survivors. Knock out one ray in five, and the remaining rays cluster into groups of four — there is the comb-teeth pattern, explained.
  • The factor 71 deletes a sparse, irregular-looking sprinkle: only \(1\) ray in \(71\). That is the occasional broken tooth — rare enough to look like a glitch, lawful enough to be a theorem.

How many rays survive in total? The count of numbers from \(1\) to \(710\) sharing no factor with \(710\). This quantity is important enough to carry a name and a symbol: Euler’s totient function \(\phi(n)\). For a product of distinct primes it peels off one factor at a time,

\[ \phi(710) = 710 \cdot \Big(1 - \tfrac{1}{2}\Big)\Big(1 - \tfrac{1}{5}\Big)\Big(1 - \tfrac{1}{71}\Big) = 280, \]

so of the \(710\) rays, exactly \(280\) can carry primes. Both mysteries are now solved: the arms come from rational approximations of \(2\pi\), and the missing ones are the residue classes that flunk a coprimality test. By the way — notice that we have rediscovered, in order, modular arithmetic, rational approximation, and the totient function, without once opening a textbook. The doodle has been a better syllabus than most syllabi.

The real question, hiding underneath

But wait — solving the two mysteries has quietly exposed a third, and it is much deeper. Look again at the right panel of the first figure. The \(280\) surviving rays don’t just contain primes; they look equally bright. No surviving ray seems privileged. Is that real? Do the primes truly spread themselves evenly across all the residue classes they are allowed to occupy?

At first glance there is no reason they should. Being coprime to \(710\) makes a ray eligible, but eligibility is not equality — the primes could have favorites. Let’s interrogate the simplest possible case: \(n = 10\). A prime bigger than \(5\) must end in \(1\), \(3\), \(7\), or \(9\) (the four digits coprime to \(10\)). Are the four endings equally popular?

P = primes_upto(2_000_000)
P = P[P > 5]
x = np.logspace(2, np.log10(2_000_000), 200)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))
for d in (1, 3, 7, 9):
    counts = np.searchsorted(P[P % 10 == d], x)
    total  = np.searchsorted(P, x)
    ax.plot(x, counts / total, lw=1.5, label=f"last digit {d}")
ax.axhline(0.25, color="gray", ls="--", lw=1)
ax.set_xscale("log"); ax.set_ylim(0.15, 0.35)
ax.set_xlabel("upper bound $x$")
ax.set_ylabel("share of primes $\\leq x$")
ax.legend(frameon=False, ncols=2)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

The early data is noisy, the way small numbers always are. But as \(x\) grows, the four shares are squeezed inexorably toward \(25\%\) each. The primes, for all their famous lawlessness, are scrupulously fair about their last digits.

This is no accident of base ten, and proving it is one of the great achievements of nineteenth-century mathematics. In 1837, Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet proved what is now called Dirichlet’s theorem on arithmetic progressions [2]: for any modulus \(n\) and any remainder \(a\) coprime to \(n\), there are infinitely many primes congruent to \(a\) mod \(n\) — and more than that, the primes distribute themselves with equal density among all \(\phi(n)\) allowed classes.1 In the language of our picture: every one of the \(280\) surviving rays carries, in the limit, exactly \(\frac{1}{280}\) of all primes. The uniform glow is a theorem.

What I find most striking is how Dirichlet proved it. The statement is as discrete as mathematics gets — counting whole numbers with no divisors, sorted by remainders. Yet the proof reaches across the aisle and grabs the machinery of continuous mathematics: infinite series, complex-valued functions (now called Dirichlet \(L\)-functions), and arguments where the key fact is that a certain function does not vanish at a certain point [3]. This crossing of the discrete–continuous border was the founding act of analytic number theory, and the border has been busy ever since: the same family of ideas — primes in residue classes, and how evenly they fill them — sits at the center of the modern assault on the twin prime conjecture, where results about bounded gaps between primes lean precisely on strong forms of equidistribution in arithmetic progressions. The question our doodle raised by accident is, quite literally, frontier mathematics.

What the doodle knew

Let’s step back and admire what just happened. We plotted primes in polar coordinates for no reason at all. The pattern that emerged was, in a strict sense, fake — the spirals belonged to all integers, the gaps to garden-variety divisibility. Not one pixel of the picture was special to the primes… except the one feature we almost took for granted: the evenness of the glow along the surviving rays, which turned out to be Dirichlet’s theorem in disguise.

And that, I think, is the real lesson, the reason to cherish pointless plots. Worked through honestly, one idle visualization forced us to rediscover residue classes, the continued-fraction approximations \(\frac{22}{7}\) and \(\frac{355}{113}\), Euler’s totient function, and the threshold of analytic number theory. Every one of those ideas also sits, definition-first and motivation-last, in a textbook chapter — where it is so easy to memorize and forget. Met this way, as answers to questions you actually asked while staring at a picture you actually cared about, they arrive as old friends. You don’t have to remember them, because now they are part of how you see.

So: go plot something for no reason. The math underneath is patient, and it knows how to introduce itself.

What to remember

The prime spiral is two theorems wearing a costume. The spiral arms are rational approximation — \(6\), then \(\frac{44}{7}\), then \(\frac{710}{113}\) chasing \(2\pi\) through its continued fraction. The missing arms are coprimality, counted by \(\phi(n)\). And the one honest mystery in the picture — that the surviving arms shine equally — is Dirichlet’s theorem: primes fill every allowed residue class with the same density, \(\frac{1}{\phi(n)}\).

The early data is noisy, the way small numbers always are. But as $x$ grows, the four shares are squeezed inexorably toward $25\%$ each. The primes, for all their famous lawlessness, are scrupulously fair about their last digits.

This is no accident of base ten, and proving it is one of the great achievements of nineteenth-century mathematics. In 1837, Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet proved what is now called **Dirichlet's theorem on arithmetic progressions** [@dirichlet1837]: for any modulus $n$ and any remainder $a$ coprime to $n$, there are infinitely many primes congruent to $a$ mod $n$ — and more than that, the primes distribute themselves with equal density among all $\phi(n)$ allowed classes.^[A note for the careful reader: what Dirichlet established is equality of *analytic density* — each allowed class gets density exactly $1/\phi(n)$ in that sense. The cleaner-sounding "natural density" version, that the *proportion* of primes up to $x$ in each allowed class tends to $1/\phi(n)$, came later, with the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions. For the picture in your head, the two say the same thing: every surviving ray is equally bright.] In the language of our picture: every one of the $280$ surviving rays carries, in the limit, exactly $\frac{1}{280}$ of all primes. The uniform glow is a theorem.

What I find most striking is *how* Dirichlet proved it. The statement is as discrete as mathematics gets — counting whole numbers with no divisors, sorted by remainders. Yet the proof reaches across the aisle and grabs the machinery of continuous mathematics: infinite series, complex-valued functions (now called Dirichlet $L$-functions), and arguments where the key fact is that a certain function does not vanish at a certain point [@apostol1976analytic]. This crossing of the discrete–continuous border was the founding act of **analytic number theory**, and the border has been busy ever since: the same family of ideas — primes in residue classes, and how evenly they fill them — sits at the center of the modern assault on the twin prime conjecture, where results about *bounded gaps between primes* lean precisely on strong forms of equidistribution in arithmetic progressions. The question our doodle raised by accident is, quite literally, frontier mathematics.

## What the doodle knew

Let's step back and admire what just happened. We plotted primes in polar coordinates for no reason at all. The pattern that emerged was, in a strict sense, fake — the spirals belonged to all integers, the gaps to garden-variety divisibility. Not one pixel of the picture was special to the primes... except the one feature we almost took for granted: the *evenness* of the glow along the surviving rays, which turned out to be Dirichlet's theorem in disguise.

And that, I think, is the real lesson, the reason to cherish pointless plots. Worked through honestly, one idle visualization forced us to rediscover residue classes, the continued-fraction approximations $\frac{22}{7}$ and $\frac{355}{113}$, Euler's totient function, and the threshold of analytic number theory. Every one of those ideas also sits, definition-first and motivation-last, in a textbook chapter — where it is so easy to memorize and forget. Met *this* way, as answers to questions you actually asked while staring at a picture you actually cared about, they arrive as old friends. You don't have to remember them, because now they are part of how you see.

So: go plot something for no reason. The math underneath is patient, and it knows how to introduce itself.

## What to remember

The prime spiral is two theorems wearing a costume. The spiral arms are rational approximation — $6$, then $\frac{44}{7}$, then $\frac{710}{113}$ chasing $2\pi$ through its continued fraction. The missing arms are coprimality, counted by $\phi(n)$. And the one honest mystery in the picture — that the surviving arms shine equally — is Dirichlet's theorem: primes fill every allowed residue class with the same density, $\frac{1}{\phi(n)}$.
