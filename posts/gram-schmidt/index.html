Gram–Schmidt: Straightening a Tangle of Vectors

How projection turns a leaning, redundant-looking set of vectors into a clean perpendicular frame, why that is secretly the QR decomposition, and a numerical experiment on where the naive version falls apart.
Published

June 3, 2026

“A mathematician, like a painter or a poet, is a maker of patterns.”

— G. H. Hardy, A Mathematician’s Apology

Imagine a handful of vectors that all lean in roughly the same direction. They span some subspace, but they describe it badly: each one carries a little of every other one inside it, and it is hard to tell what is genuinely new about each. What we would love instead is a clean frame for that same subspace, a set of directions that are perfectly perpendicular and each of unit length, so that every vector in the space splits into independent, non-overlapping pieces. Gram–Schmidt is the simple, almost obvious procedure that builds exactly such a frame, one vector at a time. And once you see the picture, the QR decomposition falls out for free.

First, the picture

Start with the very first vector and do nothing clever at all. Just keep its direction; call that your first axis. The honest work begins with the second vector.

The second vector points partly along the first axis and partly away from it. The part along the first axis is redundant, since you already have that direction. So subtract it off. Concretely, cast a shadow of the second vector onto the first axis, the orthogonal projection, and remove that shadow. What survives points in a direction the first axis could never reach: it is perpendicular to the first by construction. That perpendicular remainder becomes your second axis.

Now the pattern repeats. Bring in the third vector, look at the shadow it casts on the plane already spanned by your first two axes, subtract that shadow, and whatever remains sticks out perpendicular to both. Keep going. At every step you take the next raw vector, strip away everything your existing axes already explain, and keep only the genuinely new direction left over. Normalize each remainder to unit length and you have an orthonormal frame.

That is the whole idea. Every formula below is just bookkeeping for “subtract the shadow.”

Writing it down

Let \(v_1, \dots, v_n \in \mathbb{R}^m\) be linearly independent, and write the inner product as \(\langle x, y\rangle = x^\top y\). The projection of a vector \(v\) onto a unit vector \(q\) is \(\langle q, v\rangle\, q\): the shadow’s length times the direction. Gram–Schmidt then reads

\[ u_k = v_k - \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} \langle q_j, v_k\rangle\, q_j, \qquad q_k = \frac{u_k}{\lVert u_k \rVert}. \tag{1}\]

The sum is everything the earlier axes already account for; \(u_k\) is the leftover that they cannot. We normalize it to get the next unit axis \(q_k\).

It was QR all along

Look again at Equation 1 and rearrange it to express the original vector in terms of the new frame:

\[ v_k = \lVert u_k \rVert\, q_k + \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} \langle q_j, v_k\rangle\, q_j . \]

Read this as a recipe for rebuilding \(v_k\) out of the \(q\)’s. The crucial detail is which \(q\)’s appear: only \(q_1, \dots, q_k\), never any with a higher index. Each original vector lives entirely inside the axes built up to its own turn.

Stack the vectors as columns of a matrix \(A = [\,v_1 \mid \cdots \mid v_n\,]\) and the new axes as columns of \(Q = [\,q_1 \mid \cdots \mid q_n\,]\). The coefficients above assemble into a matrix \(R\), and because \(v_k\) uses no axis past \(q_k\), every entry of \(R\) below the diagonal is zero. We have landed on

\[ A = QR, \tag{2}\]

with \(Q\) holding orthonormal columns and \(R\) upper triangular, its diagonal entries \(r_{kk} = \lVert u_k \rVert\) all positive. The geometric act of straightening a tangle of vectors and the algebraic factorization Equation 2 are the same thing seen from two sides.

Why the frame is genuinely orthonormal

It is worth checking that the picture delivers what it promises, rather than just feeling like it should.

Theorem 1 (QR by Gram–Schmidt) If the columns of \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\) are linearly independent, then Equation 1 produces vectors \(q_1, \dots, q_n\) that are orthonormal and satisfy \(A = QR\) with \(R\) upper triangular and \(r_{kk} > 0\).

Proof. We argue by induction on \(k\) that \(q_1, \dots, q_k\) are orthonormal and span the same subspace as \(v_1, \dots, v_k\).

For \(k = 1\), \(q_1 = v_1 / \lVert v_1 \rVert\) is a single unit vector spanning the same line as \(v_1\), and \(\lVert v_1\rVert > 0\) because the columns are independent.

Assume the claim for \(k-1\). Form \(u_k\) as in Equation 1. For any earlier index \(i < k\), take the inner product with \(q_i\) and use orthonormality of the previous axes, \(\langle q_i, q_j\rangle = \delta_{ij}\): \[ \langle q_i, u_k\rangle = \langle q_i, v_k\rangle - \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} \langle q_j, v_k\rangle\,\langle q_i, q_j\rangle = \langle q_i, v_k\rangle - \langle q_i, v_k\rangle = 0 . \] So \(u_k\) is perpendicular to every previous axis. It is also nonzero: if it vanished, Equation 1 would write \(v_k\) as a combination of \(q_1, \dots, q_{k-1}\), hence of \(v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}\), contradicting linear independence. Therefore \(q_k = u_k / \lVert u_k\rVert\) is a unit vector orthogonal to all earlier ones, and since \(u_k\) is \(v_k\) plus a combination of the previous axes, the spans still agree. The induction closes. Collecting the coefficients into \(R\) gives Equation 2, and \(r_{kk} = \lVert u_k\rVert > 0\). \(\qquad\blacksquare\)

Where the naive version breaks

Here the story takes a turn that pure mathematics alone would never warn you about. On paper, Equation 1 is flawless. On a computer, the order in which you subtract the shadows turns out to matter enormously.

There are two ways to organize the subtraction. Classical Gram–Schmidt computes every projection against the original vector \(v_k\) and removes them together. Modified Gram–Schmidt removes each shadow one at a time, always projecting the running remainder that is left so far. Algebraically the two are identical. In floating-point arithmetic they are not, because the modified version always works with the most up-to-date, mostly-perpendicular remainder, and so it never lets rounding errors pile up unexamined.

To see the gap, feed both versions a deliberately nasty matrix: the \(8 \times 8\) Hilbert matrix, whose columns are so nearly parallel that its condition number runs into the billions.

import numpy as np

def gram_schmidt(A, modified):
    m, n = A.shape
    Q = np.zeros((m, n))
    R = np.zeros((n, n))
    for k in range(n):
        v = A[:, k].copy()
        for j in range(k):
            # classical projects the original column; modified the running remainder
            R[j, k] = Q[:, j] @ (v if modified else A[:, k])
            v = v - R[j, k] * Q[:, j]
        R[k, k] = np.linalg.norm(v)
        Q[:, k] = v / R[k, k]
    return Q, R

n = 8
H = np.array([[1.0 / (i + j + 1) for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)])
print(f"condition number of H: {np.linalg.cond(H):.2e}\n")

for name, modified in [("classical", False), ("modified", True)]:
    Q, R = gram_schmidt(H, modified)
    orthogonality = np.linalg.norm(Q.T @ Q - np.eye(n))
    reconstruction = np.linalg.norm(H - Q @ R)
    print(f"{name:9s}:  |Q^T Q - I| = {orthogonality:.2e}    |H - QR| = {reconstruction:.2e}")

Qh, _ = np.linalg.qr(H)   # numpy uses Householder reflections
print(f"{'numpy.qr':9s}:  |Q^T Q - I| = {np.linalg.norm(Qh.T @ Qh - np.eye(n)):.2e}")
condition number of H: 1.53e+10

classical:  |Q^T Q - I| = 1.42e+00    |H - QR| = 9.71e-17
modified :  |Q^T Q - I| = 3.62e-07    |H - QR| = 1.07e-16
numpy.qr :  |Q^T Q - I| = 1.46e-15

Both methods rebuild \(H\) from \(Q\) and \(R\) essentially perfectly, with \(\lVert H - QR\rVert\) down at roundoff. That is not where the danger lives. Look instead at \(\lVert Q^\top Q - I\rVert\), the honest test of whether the columns of \(Q\) really are orthonormal. Classical Gram–Schmidt scores around \(1\), meaning its “orthonormal” axes are nothing of the sort, they have collapsed back toward each other. The modified version holds the error near \(10^{-7}\), and the Householder-based routine in NumPy reaches full machine precision around \(10^{-15}\).

The lesson is not that Gram–Schmidt is wrong. It is that a formula can be exactly true and still be the wrong thing to type, because finite-precision arithmetic has a geometry of its own. When you actually need a stable orthonormal frame, reach for modified Gram–Schmidt or, better still, a Householder QR like the one inside numpy.linalg.qr. A careful account of all this lives in [1].

What to remember

The heart of Gram–Schmidt is a single move repeated: take the next vector, subtract the shadow your existing axes already explain, and keep the perpendicular remainder. That move, written out, is the factorization \(A = QR\), with the orthonormal frame in \(Q\) and the bookkeeping in an upper-triangular \(R\). The proof that the frame is truly orthonormal is a short induction resting on one cancellation. And the moment you run it, a quieter truth appears: on a real machine the naive ordering can throw the orthogonality away entirely, so the version you compute should be the modified one, or Householder if you can afford it.
