Quaternions: Seeing Four Dimensions with Three-Dimensional Eyes

pure
algebra
geometry
Why a number system for space is forced to have four dimensions, how Hamilton’s product secretly contains the dot and cross products, and how to watch quaternion multiplication — a double rotation of a four-dimensional sphere — with ordinary eyes.
Published

June 11, 2026

“Here as he walked by on the 16th of October 1843, Sir William Rowan Hamilton, in a flash of genius, discovered the fundamental formula for quaternion multiplication \(i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1\) & cut it on a stone of this bridge.”

— plaque on Broom Bridge, Dublin

Quaternions have a public relations problem that is almost two centuries old. They are four-dimensional numbers, which already sounds like a provocation; their multiplication refuses to commute, which feels like a betrayal; and the standard introduction — here is a table, \(ij = k\), \(ji = -k\), memorize it — manages to make one of the most geometric objects in mathematics feel like an arbitrary list of rules. If you have met quaternions before and bounced off, the problem was almost certainly not you.

This post makes two promises. The first is about logic: I want to show you that almost nothing about the quaternions is arbitrary — not the strange dimension count, not the multiplication rules, not even the failure of commutativity. Each is forced, step by inevitable step, the moment you ask one innocent question about multiplying points in space. The second is about sight: by the end, I want you to be able to read a product like \(i \cdot j = k\) and watch a specific, rigid motion of a four-dimensional sphere, using nothing but your three-dimensional imagination.

A flash of genius on a Dublin bridge

By the 1830s, mathematicians had made peace with complex numbers, and more than peace — they had fallen in love. A number \(a + bi\) is a point in the plane, and multiplication by it rotates and stretches that plane. Algebra and plane geometry turned out to be two languages for one thing. The Irish mathematician William Rowan Hamilton was gripped by the obvious next question: we live in three dimensions, so where is the number system for space?

He searched for over a decade. The story goes that his sons would ask at breakfast, “Papa, can you multiply triplets?” and he would shake his head: “No, I can only add and subtract them.” Then, on October 16th, 1843, walking along the Royal Canal, he saw it in a flash: the answer was not to add one imaginary axis to the complex numbers but two — three imaginary units \(i\), \(j\), \(k\) for space, bound to a real axis standing perpendicular to all of them. He scratched the governing law into the stone of Broom Bridge,

\[ i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1, \tag{1}\]

and announced the discovery to the world within weeks [1]. He called the new numbers quaternions, and what followed is one of the great turf wars in the history of mathematics. Hamilton — and after him an actual official society — pushed quaternions as the language of three-dimensional science. The opposition produced some genuinely magnificent trash talk: Lord Kelvin wrote that quaternions, “though beautifully ingenious, have been an unmixed evil to those who have touched them in any way.” There is even a theory, taken half-seriously by historians, that the Mad Hatter’s endless rotation around the tea table — written, remember, by an Oxford mathematician — is a satire of their order-sensitive multiplication.

The vector calculus of Gibbs and Heaviside won the textbooks, and we will see in a moment that it won them by strip-mining Hamilton’s formula. But the twentieth century staged two rescues: computer graphics and robotics rediscovered that quaternions encode 3D rotations more compactly and stably than anything else — your phone and every spacecraft attitude controller multiply them constantly — and quantum mechanics found that the algebra of a spinning electron is Hamilton’s algebra in a lab coat. Numbers this inevitable do not stay buried.

So let’s do what Hamilton could not do on the bridge, with no flash of genius required: walk the logic slowly enough that four dimensions become the obvious answer.

The innocent question

What should “multiplying points” even mean? Let’s write down our demands honestly, because everything follows from them. We have points in some \(n\)-dimensional space, we can already add them and scale them, and we want a multiplication such that, first, it distributes over addition the way any self-respecting product does; second, we can divide — every nonzero point has a reciprocal; and third, it respects geometry. That last demand has a precise, modest form. Every point \(q\) has a size \(|q|\), its distance from the origin, and we ask that

\[ |q_1 q_2| \;=\; |q_1|\,|q_2|. \tag{2}\]

Lengths multiply. That is all. It looks like a throwaway technicality, and it is in fact the steel beam holding up everything in this post.

Watch what Equation 2 already does in two dimensions. Write the squared length of \((a,b)\) as \(a^2 + b^2\). If a length-respecting multiplication exists, then the product of two sums of two squares must again be a sum of two squares — and it is, by an identity that was known to Diophantus seventeen centuries before complex numbers existed:

\[ (a^2 + b^2)(c^2 + d^2) \;=\; (ac - bd)^2 + (ad + bc)^2. \tag{3}\]

Now look closely at the right-hand side. The expressions \(ac - bd\) and \(ad + bc\) are precisely the real and imaginary parts of the product \((a + bi)(c + di)\). The multiplication rule of complex numbers is not a convention someone chose; it is the unique way to factor the two-square identity. The algebra was hiding inside an arithmetic fact all along. Take a moment with that — it is the cleanest example I know of a number system being discovered rather than invented.

Why three dimensions refuse

So Hamilton’s program needs a three-square identity: the product of two sums of three squares should be a sum of three squares. And here the universe says no, and it says it with a concrete counterexample rather than abstract hand-wringing. Both \(3 = 1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2\) and \(5 = 0^2 + 1^2 + 2^2\) are sums of three squares. Their product is \(15\) — and \(15\) simply is not. Try it: \(9 + 4 + 1\) overshoots at \(14\), every other combination misses too. (Number theorists know the full story: integers of the form \(8b + 7\) refuse to be three squares, and \(15\) is the smallest product to weaponize this.)

Sit with what just happened. A fact about the integer \(15\) reaches up and forbids an entire algebra. No bilinear, length-respecting multiplication of 3D points can exist, no matter how clever you are, because if it did, Equation 2 would manufacture a three-square identity and \(15\) stands in the way. Hamilton’s decade of frustration was not a failure of imagination — he was pushing against a theorem.

But Euler, a century before Hamilton, had found that four squares work [2]:

\[ (a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2)(e^2+f^2+g^2+h^2) = (ae-bf-cg-dh)^2 + (af+be+ch-dg)^2 + (ag-bh+ce+df)^2 + (ah+bg-cf+de)^2. \tag{4}\]

At first glance this looks like a wall of symbols, but you have already seen its little sibling Equation 3 and you know how to read it: those four expressions on the right are a multiplication rule trying to be born. Factor the four-square identity the way the two-square identity factors, and the structure constants of Equation 1 fall out — not chosen, forced. The quaternions had been waiting inside Euler’s identity since 1748, ninety-five years before Hamilton heard them knocking. And the pattern stops where it stops for a reason: Hurwitz proved in 1898 that such identities exist only for \(n = 1, 2, 4, 8\) — reals, complexes, quaternions, and (by the way, with division but at the price of associativity) the eight-dimensional octonions, after which the door closes forever [2]. The dimension four was never strange. It was one of the only four options the integers ever put on the table.

The product that contains a geometry course

Now let’s actually hold the multiplication in our hands, because it has more inside it than its definition suggests. Split a quaternion into Hamilton’s two parts: a real number plus a 3D point, \(q = a + \mathbf{v}\). Hamilton needed a name for that pure spatial part, the piece spanned by \(i, j, k\), and the word he coined was vector — the everyday word is a fossil of this exact construction. Multiply two quaternions \((a + \mathbf{v})(b + \mathbf{w})\) by brute distribution using Equation 1, collect terms, and the result organizes itself into something startling:

\[ (a + \mathbf{v})(b + \mathbf{w}) \;=\; \underbrace{ab - \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w}}_{\text{real part}} \;+\; \underbrace{a\,\mathbf{w} + b\,\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w}}_{\text{vector part}}. \tag{5}\]

Look at what surfaced: the dot product and the cross product, both of them, with their signs and their geometry, born as the real and vector shrapnel of a single quaternion product. This is not an analogy — it is ancestry. Gibbs and Heaviside built vector analysis by taking Equation 5 apart and keeping the pieces; the dot and cross products you learned as separate gadgets are the two halves of one multiplication that predates them both. Doesn’t that just feel beautiful? Every identity that mixes dot and cross products is secretly a statement about associativity in Hamilton’s algebra.

And the famous rules are now corollaries you can see. For pure vectors the real parts vanish, so \(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{w} = -\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w}\). Take \(\mathbf{v} = i\), \(\mathbf{w} = j\): perpendicular, so the dot part is zero, and \(i \times j = k\). Hence \(ij = k\). Swap the order and the cross product flips sign: \(ji = -k\). The notorious non-commutativity of quaternions is nothing but the antisymmetry of the cross product — which is itself the honest geometric fact that orientation matters in space. A number system that truly speaks for 3D geometry had no choice but to give up commutativity; a commutative one would have been lying about space. Even \(\mathbf{v}^2 = -|\mathbf{v}|^2\), the rule that every unit vector is a square root of \(-1\), falls out of Equation 5 in one line.

That last sentence deserves its own paragraph, because it quietly hands us the entire shape of what comes next. Every unit vector \(\mathbf{u}\) — not just \(i\) — satisfies \(\mathbf{u}^2 = -1\). So inside the quaternions, the plane spanned by \(1\) and \(\mathbf{u}\) is a perfect copy of the complex numbers, and every unit quaternion can be written as

\[ q \;=\; \cos\theta + \mathbf{u}\sin\theta, \tag{6}\]

an “\(e^{i\theta}\)” with a choice of which \(i\). The unit quaternions are a book whose pages are complex unit circles, all bound together along the spine \(\{1, -1\}\), one page for every direction \(\mathbf{u}\) in space.

The shape of the unit quaternions

Where do those unit quaternions live? In four coordinates \((a,b,c,d)\) with \(a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2 = 1\): the unit hypersphere \(S^3\), a three-dimensional surface curving through four-dimensional space. And Equation 2 gives this sphere a property that ordinary spheres can only envy: the product of two unit quaternions is again a unit quaternion. The hypersphere is closed under multiplication — it is a group, a space whose every point is also a motion of that space. Among all spheres of all dimensions, essentially only the circle \(S^1\) and this hypersphere \(S^3\) carry such a structure. The circle’s multiplication runs the theory of angles and Fourier series; its big sibling’s multiplication runs the theory of spatial rotation. We have walked into very select company.

So the object of study is fixed: a 4D sphere we cannot see, on which multiplication acts by — what, exactly? By Equation 2, multiplying by a unit quaternion preserves all lengths: it is a rigid rotation of four-dimensional space. The two promises of this post have become one task. See the hypersphere; watch it turn.

Borrowing eyes

Here is the move, and it is a move worth owning for life: when a space has one dimension too many for your eyes, ask how you would show your world to a creature with one dimension too few — then accept the same charity from above. Mathematicians have a precise instrument for this, stereographic projection, and we will rehearse it twice before using it in earnest.

Rehearsal one: show a circle to a creature who knows only a line. The unit complex numbers form a circle; rotation is its native motion; and a rotation has just one degree of freedom, so a line ought to suffice to display it. Project from the point \(-1\): for each other point of the circle, draw the straight line from \(-1\) through it and mark where it crosses the tangent line at the opposite pole. The point \(1\) lands at the center; \(i\) and \(-i\) stay put; arcs near \(-1\) fly off toward the two infinite ends, and \(-1\) itself becomes the point at infinity. Now multiply the circle by \(i\) — a crisp quarter turn — and watch its shadow: the center point slides to \(i\), the point at \(i\) flees off the end of the line, infinity wraps around and re-enters at \(-i\), and four repetitions bring every point home: \(i^4 = 1\), visible. The motion looks stretchy and strange, but that is the lens, not the motion. Holding those two truths at once — the shadow distorts, the object is rigid — is the entire skill.

Rehearsal two: show a rotating sphere to a creature who knows only a plane, and — this is the version we need — build the sphere out of number axes: real axis vertical, two imaginary axes \(i, j\) spanning the floor. Project the unit sphere from its south pole \(-1\) onto the floor. The north pole \(1\) lands at the origin. The equator through \(\pm i, \pm j\) is already in the floor and does not move: one honest, undistorted circle amid the shadows. The northern hemisphere (positive real part) lands inside it, the southern outside, and \(-1\) is again the point at infinity. One dictionary entry pays for the whole rehearsal: every straight line the flat creature sees is the shadow of a circle through \(-1\). A line is a circle through infinity. Once that clicks, a rotation that appears to deform a circle into a line stops being alarming — upstairs it is just a circle turning rigidly through the south pole. Spin the sphere a quarter turn about the \(j\)-axis (\(1 \to i \to -1 \to -i\)) and the flat creature sees, embedded along one line of their floor, exactly the lurching motion of rehearsal one, while the honest circle swings up into a vertical line. Nothing stretched. It never does.

Now the real thing, with practiced eyes. Project the hypersphere of unit quaternions from \(-1\) into our 3D space. The number \(1\) lands at the center of the room. The “equator” — all unit quaternions with zero real part, which is exactly the sphere of unit vectors through \(i, j, k\) — lies in our space already and projects to itself: an honest unit sphere around the origin, the one fully trustworthy object in view. Unit quaternions with positive real part fill the inside of that sphere; negative real parts land outside; \(-1\) is the point at infinity, in every direction at once. And the dictionary carries up a dimension: every plane through the origin is secretly a great sphere through \(-1\); every line through the origin is secretly a great circle through \(-1\). The line you see through \(1\), \(i\), \(-i\) is the unit circle of the complex numbers — one page of the book from Equation 6, lying open in the room. Pause here until the sentence “everything I see is at distance exactly \(1\) from a number zero that is nowhere in the picture” stops hurting. It does stop.

Watching the multiplication

What does left-multiplication by a unit quaternion do to this picture? Here is the answer, and every clause of it is now checkable with your own eyes:

Multiplying on the left by \(q = \cos\theta + \mathbf{u}\sin\theta\) rotates the complex circle of \(\mathbf{u}\) — the circle through \(1\), \(\mathbf{u}\), \(-1\), seen by us as a line through the origin — by the angle \(\theta\), carrying \(1\) to \(q\). Simultaneously, it rotates the great circle perpendicular to it, lying on the honest sphere, by the same angle \(\theta\).

Two completely perpendicular circles — same center, same radius, no common point, a configuration that sounds impossible until you grant them four dimensions to dodge each other in — turning in lockstep. Rotations with this equal-angle property are called isoclinic, and they exist only in four dimensions and above. In 3D every rotation has an axis, a line of points left alone; this motion fixes nothing whatsoever. (I first learned to actually see this picture, with the projected hypersphere and the hand gestures below, from Grant Sanderson’s video on the subject [3] — the debt for the visual language of this section is his.)

Make it concrete with \(q = i\), so \(\mathbf{u} = i\) and \(\theta = 90^\circ\). The circle through \(1\) and \(i\) appears as a line through the origin, and along it runs the lurch you have now rehearsed twice: \(1 \to i\), \(i \to -1\) (off to infinity), \(-1 \to -i\) (back in from beyond), \(-i \to 1\). Meanwhile the perpendicular great circle through \(j\) and \(k\), sitting on the honest sphere, turns \(90^\circ\) in the direction given by a right-hand rule: point your right thumb from \(1\) toward \(i\) along the visible line, and your fingers curl the way the \(jk\)-circle rolls. Read the products straight off the motion:

\[ i \cdot 1 = i, \qquad i \cdot i = -1, \qquad i \cdot j = k, \qquad i \cdot k = -j. \]

My honest hope is that this is no longer a table but a film: the line flowing, the circle rolling with your curled fingers, four frames returning everything home because \(i^4 = 1\). And since multiplication distributes, knowing the motion on the basis \(1, i, j, k\) pins it down on every quaternion — see the action on four points, know it on the whole 4D space. For a general \(q\) the recipe never changes: draw the circle through \(1\), \(q\), \(-1\); turn it until \(1\) reaches \(q\); turn the perpendicular circle on the honest sphere by the same angle, right-handedly. One point of the hypersphere chosen, one rigid motion determined. The two computations \(ij = k\) and \(ji = -k\) are now two genuinely different films — thumb toward \(i\) curling \(j\) up to \(k\), versus thumb toward \(j\) curling \(i\) down to \(-k\) — and non-commutativity is something your hands knew before your algebra did. It is the same lesson the cross product whispered earlier, now playing on the big screen.

Two hands, all of rotation

One refinement opens the door to everything quaternions are used for. You can also multiply by \(q\) on the right, and the motion is the mirror twin: the same circle through \(1, q, -1\) turns the same way, but the perpendicular circle now obeys a left-hand rule. Left multiplications and right multiplications are two distinct families of isoclinic rotations — and here is the theorem that I find quietly astonishing: composing them, \(x \mapsto p\,x\,q\), produces every rotation of four-dimensional space, and essentially uniquely [2]. The entire rotation group of 4D, an object you might expect to be hopelessly opaque, factors into a left hand and a right hand, each one a copy of our hypersphere.

And now you can feel the next chapter coming. A single multiplication tilts the honest sphere of vectors off through the fourth dimension — beautiful, but not yet a rotation of space. The fix is to let the two hands collaborate against each other: the sandwich \(\mathbf{v} \mapsto q\,\mathbf{v}\,q^{-1}\), one left action and one right action whose off-sphere tilts cancel exactly, leaving the honest sphere turning rigidly in place. That sandwich is how your phone, your game engine, and your favorite spacecraft actually compute rotations, and it hides one more elegant secret — \(q\) and \(-q\) perform the same rotation, a two-to-one disguise called the double cover, which is precisely the mathematics of electron spin. Unpacking it properly deserves its own post, and it will get one.

Until then, sit with what the innocent question bought us: lengths multiply, so \(15\) forbids three dimensions, so Euler’s identity forces four; the product, once forced, births the dot and cross products and pays for non-commutativity with honest geometry; the unit quaternions close into one of the only spheres in mathematics that is also a group; and its multiplication, seen through a projection we borrowed by climbing down two dimensions and back up, is a pair of perpendicular circles turning as one. If you want to hold the hypersphere in your own hands, Sanderson and Eater built an explorable version of these pictures that you can drag around yourself [4] — go play. The “unmixed evil” was geometry all along, waiting a century and a half for someone to draw it.

## A flash of genius on a Dublin bridge

A number $a + bi$ is a point in the plane, and multiplication by it *rotates and stretches* that plane. Algebra and plane geometry turned out to be two languages for one thing. ## The innocent question

What should "multiplying points" even mean? Let's write down our demands honestly, because everything follows from them. We have points in some $n$-dimensional space, we can already add them and scale them, and we want a multiplication such that, first, it distributes over addition the way any self-respecting product does; second, we can *divide* — every nonzero point has a reciprocal; and third, it respects geometry. That last demand has a precise, modest form. Every point $q$ has a size $|q|$, its distance from the origin, and we ask that

$$
|q_1 q_2| \;=\; |q_1|\,|q_2|.
$$ {#eq-norm}

Lengths multiply. That is all. It looks like a throwaway technicality, and it is in fact the steel beam holding up everything in this post.

Watch what @eq-norm already does in two dimensions. Write the squared length of $(a,b)$ as $a^2 + b^2$. If a length-respecting multiplication exists, then the product of two sums of two squares must again *be* a sum of two squares — and it is, by an identity that was known to Diophantus seventeen centuries before complex numbers existed:

$$
(a^2 + b^2)(c^2 + d^2) \;=\; (ac - bd)^2 + (ad + bc)^2.
$$ {#eq-twosquare}

Now look closely at the right-hand side. The expressions $ac - bd$ and $ad + bc$ are precisely the real and imaginary parts of the product $(a + bi)(c + di)$. The multiplication rule of complex numbers is not a convention someone chose; it is the *unique way to factor the two-square identity*. The algebra was hiding inside an arithmetic fact all along. Take a moment with that — it is the cleanest example I know of a number system being discovered rather than invented.

## Why three dimensions refuse

So Hamilton's program needs a *three*-square identity: the product of two sums of three squares should be a sum of three squares. And here the universe says no, and it says it with a concrete counterexample rather than abstract hand-wringing. Both $3 = 1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2$ and $5 = 0^2 + 1^2 + 2^2$ are sums of three squares. Their product is $15$ — and $15$ simply is not. Try it: $9 + 4 + 1$ overshoots at $14$, every other combination misses too. (Number theorists know the full story: integers of the form $8b + 7$ refuse to be three squares, and $15$ is the smallest product to weaponize this.)

Sit with what just happened. A fact about the integer $15$ reaches up and forbids an entire algebra. No bilinear, length-respecting multiplication of 3D points can exist, no matter how clever you are, because if it did, @eq-norm would manufacture a three-square identity and $15$ stands in the way. Hamilton's decade of frustration was not a failure of imagination — he was pushing against a theorem.

But Euler, a century before Hamilton, had found that *four* squares work [@conway2003]:

$$

$$ {#eq-foursquare}

At first glance this looks like a wall of symbols, but you have already seen its little sibling @eq-twosquare and you know how to read it: *those four expressions on the right are a multiplication rule trying to be born.* Factor the four-square identity the way the two-square identity factors, and the structure constants of @eq-hamilton fall out — not chosen, *forced*. ## The product that contains a geometry course

Now let's actually hold the multiplication in our hands, because it has more inside it than its definition suggests. Split a quaternion into Hamilton's two parts: a real number plus a 3D point, $q = a + \mathbf{v}$. Hamilton needed a name for that pure spatial part, the piece spanned by $i, j, k$, and the word he coined was *vector* — the everyday word is a fossil of this exact construction. Multiply two quaternions $(a + \mathbf{v})(b + \mathbf{w})$ by brute distribution using @eq-hamilton, collect terms, and the result organizes itself into something startling:

$$
(a + \mathbf{v})(b + \mathbf{w}) \;=\; \underbrace{ab - \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w}}_{\text{real part}} \;+\; \underbrace{a\,\mathbf{w} + b\,\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w}}_{\text{vector part}}.
$$ {#eq-product}

Look at what surfaced: the dot product and the cross product, both of them, with their signs and their geometry, born as the real and vector shrapnel of a single quaternion product. This is not an analogy — it is ancestry. Gibbs and Heaviside built vector analysis by taking @eq-product apart and keeping the pieces; the dot and cross products you learned as separate gadgets are the two halves of one multiplication that predates them both. Doesn't that just feel beautiful? Every identity that mixes dot and cross products is secretly a statement about associativity in Hamilton's algebra.

And the famous rules are now corollaries you can see. For pure vectors the real parts vanish, so $\mathbf{v}\mathbf{w} = -\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w}$. Take $\mathbf{v} = i$, $\mathbf{w} = j$: perpendicular, so the dot part is zero, and $i \times j = k$. Hence $ij = k$. Swap the order and the cross product flips sign: $ji = -k$. The notorious non-commutativity of quaternions is nothing but the antisymmetry of the cross product — which is itself the honest geometric fact that orientation matters in space. A number system that *truly* speaks for 3D geometry had no choice but to give up commutativity; a commutative one would have been lying about space. Even $\mathbf{v}^2 = -|\mathbf{v}|^2$, the rule that every unit vector is a square root of $-1$, falls out of @eq-product in one line.

That last sentence deserves its own paragraph, because it quietly hands us the entire shape of what comes next. *Every* unit vector $\mathbf{u}$ — not just $i$ — satisfies $\mathbf{u}^2 = -1$. So inside the quaternions, the plane spanned by $1$ and $\mathbf{u}$ is a perfect copy of the complex numbers, and every unit quaternion can be written as

$$
q \;=\; \cos\theta + \mathbf{u}\sin\theta,
$$ {#eq-polar}

an "$e^{i\theta}$" with a *choice of which $i$*. The unit quaternions are a book whose pages are complex unit circles, all bound together along the spine $\{1, -1\}$, one page for every direction $\mathbf{u}$ in space.

## The shape of the unit quaternions

Where do those unit quaternions live? In four coordinates $(a,b,c,d)$ with $a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2 = 1$: the unit *hypersphere* $S^3$, a three-dimensional surface curving through four-dimensional space. And @eq-norm gives this sphere a property that ordinary spheres can only envy: the product of two unit quaternions is again a unit quaternion. The hypersphere is *closed under multiplication* — it is a group, a space whose every point is also a motion of that space. Among all spheres of all dimensions, essentially only the circle $S^1$ and this hypersphere $S^3$ carry such a structure. The circle's multiplication runs the theory of angles and Fourier series; its big sibling's multiplication runs the theory of spatial rotation. We have walked into very select company.

So the object of study is fixed: a 4D sphere we cannot see, on which multiplication acts by — what, exactly? By @eq-norm, multiplying by a unit quaternion preserves all lengths: it is a *rigid rotation of four-dimensional space*. The two promises of this post have become one task. See the hypersphere; watch it turn.

## Borrowing eyes

Here is the move, and it is a move worth owning for life: when a space has one dimension too many for your eyes, ask how you would show *your* world to a creature with one dimension too few — then accept the same charity from above. Mathematicians have a precise instrument for this, **stereographic projection**, and we will rehearse it twice before using it in earnest.

Rehearsal one: show a circle to a creature who knows only a line. The unit complex numbers form a circle; rotation is its native motion; and a rotation has just *one* degree of freedom, so a line ought to suffice to display it. Project from the point $-1$: for each other point of the circle, draw the straight line from $-1$ through it and mark where it crosses the tangent line at the opposite pole. The point $1$ lands at the center; $i$ and $-i$ stay put; arcs near $-1$ fly off toward the two infinite ends, and $-1$ itself becomes the *point at infinity*. Now multiply the circle by $i$ — a crisp quarter turn — and watch its shadow: the center point slides to $i$, the point at $i$ flees off the end of the line, infinity wraps around and re-enters at $-i$, and four repetitions bring every point home: $i^4 = 1$, visible. The motion looks stretchy and strange, but that is the lens, not the motion. Holding those two truths at once — *the shadow distorts, the object is rigid* — is the entire skill.

Rehearsal two: show a rotating sphere to a creature who knows only a plane, and — this is the version we need — build the sphere out of number axes: real axis vertical, two imaginary axes $i, j$ spanning the floor. Project the unit sphere from its south pole $-1$ onto the floor. The north pole $1$ lands at the origin. The equator through $\pm i, \pm j$ *is already in the floor* and does not move: one honest, undistorted circle amid the shadows. The northern hemisphere (positive real part) lands inside it, the southern outside, and $-1$ is again the point at infinity. One dictionary entry pays for the whole rehearsal: every straight line the flat creature sees is the shadow of a circle through $-1$. *A line is a circle through infinity.* Once that clicks, a rotation that appears to deform a circle into a line stops being alarming — upstairs it is just a circle turning rigidly through the south pole. Spin the sphere a quarter turn about the $j$-axis ($1 \to i \to -1 \to -i$) and the flat creature sees, embedded along one line of their floor, exactly the lurching motion of rehearsal one, while the honest circle swings up into a vertical line. Nothing stretched. It never does.

Now the real thing, with practiced eyes. Project the hypersphere of unit quaternions from $-1$ into our 3D space. The number $1$ lands at the center of the room. The "equator" — all unit quaternions with zero real part, which is exactly the sphere of unit *vectors* through $i, j, k$ — lies in our space already and projects to itself: an honest unit sphere around the origin, the one fully trustworthy object in view. Unit quaternions with positive real part fill the inside of that sphere; negative real parts land outside; $-1$ is the point at infinity, in every direction at once. And the dictionary carries up a dimension: every plane through the origin is secretly a great sphere through $-1$; every line through the origin is secretly a great circle through $-1$. The line you see through $1$, $i$, $-i$ *is* the unit circle of the complex numbers — one page of the book from @eq-polar, lying open in the room. Pause here until the sentence "everything I see is at distance exactly $1$ from a number zero that is nowhere in the picture" stops hurting. It does stop.

## Watching the multiplication

What does left-multiplication by a unit quaternion do to this picture? Here is the answer, and every clause of it is now checkable with your own eyes:

> Multiplying on the left by $q = \cos\theta + \mathbf{u}\sin\theta$ rotates the complex circle of $\mathbf{u}$ — the circle through $1$, $\mathbf{u}$, $-1$, seen by us as a line through the origin — by the angle $\theta$, carrying $1$ to $q$. *Simultaneously*, it rotates the great circle perpendicular to it, lying on the honest sphere, by the **same** angle $\theta$.

Two completely perpendicular circles — same center, same radius, no common point, a configuration that sounds impossible until you grant them four dimensions to dodge each other in — turning in lockstep. Rotations with this equal-angle property are called *isoclinic*, and they exist only in four dimensions and above. In 3D every rotation has an axis, a line of points left alone; this motion fixes nothing whatsoever. (I first learned to actually *see* this picture, with the projected hypersphere and the hand gestures below, from Grant Sanderson's video on the subject [@sanderson2018quaternions] — the debt for the visual language of this section is his.)

Make it concrete with $q = i$, so $\mathbf{u} = i$ and $\theta = 90^\circ$. The circle through $1$ and $i$ appears as a line through the origin, and along it runs the lurch you have now rehearsed twice: $1 \to i$, $i \to -1$ (off to infinity), $-1 \to -i$ (back in from beyond), $-i \to 1$. Meanwhile the perpendicular great circle through $j$ and $k$, sitting on the honest sphere, turns $90^\circ$ in the direction given by a right-hand rule: point your right thumb from $1$ toward $i$ along the visible line, and your fingers curl the way the $jk$-circle rolls. Read the products straight off the motion:

$$
i \cdot 1 = i, \qquad i \cdot i = -1, \qquad i \cdot j = k, \qquad i \cdot k = -j.
$$

My honest hope is that this is no longer a table but a film: the line flowing, the circle rolling with your curled fingers, four frames returning everything home because $i^4 = 1$. And since multiplication distributes, knowing the motion on the basis $1, i, j, k$ pins it down on every quaternion — see the action on four points, know it on the whole 4D space. For a general $q$ the recipe never changes: draw the circle through $1$, $q$, $-1$; turn it until $1$ reaches $q$; turn the perpendicular circle on the honest sphere by the same angle, right-handedly. One point of the hypersphere chosen, one rigid motion determined. The two computations $ij = k$ and $ji = -k$ are now two genuinely different films — thumb toward $i$ curling $j$ up to $k$, versus thumb toward $j$ curling $i$ down to $-k$ — and non-commutativity is something your hands knew before your algebra did. It is the same lesson the cross product whispered earlier, now playing on the big screen.

## Two hands, all of rotation

One refinement opens the door to everything quaternions are used for. You can also multiply by $q$ on the *right*, and the motion is the mirror twin: the same circle through $1, q, -1$ turns the same way, but the perpendicular circle now obeys a *left*-hand rule. Left multiplications and right multiplications are two distinct families of isoclinic rotations — and here is the theorem that I find quietly astonishing: composing them, $x \mapsto p\,x\,q$, produces *every* rotation of four-dimensional space, and essentially uniquely [@conway2003]. The entire rotation group of 4D, an object you might expect to be hopelessly opaque, factors into a left hand and a right hand, each one a copy of our hypersphere.

And now you can feel the next chapter coming. A single multiplication tilts the honest sphere of vectors off through the fourth dimension — beautiful, but not yet a rotation of *space*. The fix is to let the two hands collaborate against each other: the sandwich $\mathbf{v} \mapsto q\,\mathbf{v}\,q^{-1}$, one left action and one right action whose off-sphere tilts cancel exactly, leaving the honest sphere turning rigidly in place. That sandwich is how your phone, your game engine, and your favorite spacecraft actually compute rotations, and it hides one more elegant secret — $q$ and $-q$ perform the *same* rotation, a two-to-one disguise called the double cover, which is precisely the mathematics of electron spin. Unpacking it properly deserves its own post, and it will get one.

Until then, sit with what the innocent question bought us: lengths multiply, so $15$ forbids three dimensions, so Euler's identity forces four; the product, once forced, births the dot and cross products and pays for non-commutativity with honest geometry; the unit quaternions close into one of the only spheres in mathematics that is also a group; and its multiplication, seen through a projection we borrowed by climbing down two dimensions and back up, is a pair of perpendicular circles turning as one. If you want to hold the hypersphere in your own hands, Sanderson and Eater built an explorable version of these pictures that you can drag around yourself [@eater2018quaternions] — go play. The "unmixed evil" was geometry all along, waiting a century and a half for someone to draw it.
