How a sliding overlap counts dice sums, multiplies polynomials, blurs images, describes system memory, and lets Fourier transforms turn a slow calculation into pointwise multiplication.
Published

June 18, 2026

“Mathematics is the art of giving the same name to different things.”

– Henri Poincare, traditionally attributed

Convolution has a public-relations problem. It is the operation behind dice sums, image blur, audio filters, polynomial multiplication, differential equations, and the fast Fourier transform, but the first time many people meet it, it looks like a cryptic sum with one index running backward.

So let us postpone the symbols for a moment.

Here is the picture I want you to carry instead.

Imagine two transparent strips laid over the same number line. One strip has dark ink printed on it: a heavy patch means “this position matters a lot,” a light patch means “this position barely matters.” Now flip the second strip around, slide it across the first, and at each position ask:

How much ink is overlapping ink?

Where the dark parts meet, the answer is large. Where a dark part meets a blank part, the answer is small. As you slide, the overlap swells, peaks, and fades. That sliding score is convolution. The formula, when it finally appears, is only the receipt for this sliding overlap.

I first learned to see this operation from Grant Sanderson’s 3Blue1Brown video on the subject [1]. What follows is an attempt to rebuild the picture from scratch, slowly enough that the formula stops feeling like a definition and starts feeling like the shadow cast by a moving stencil.

The toy version: two dice

Let us begin somewhere almost insultingly concrete. Roll two fair dice. A total of \(2\) is rare because there is only one way to get it: \[ (1,1). \] A total of \(7\) is common because six pairs land there: \[ (1,6),\ (2,5),\ (3,4),\ (4,3),\ (5,2),\ (6,1). \]

The familiar count is \[ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 5,\ 6,\ 5,\ 4,\ 3,\ 2,\ 1. \]

That pyramid is already a convolution. One die contributes a number. The other die contributes a number. For a target total \(k\), we ask:

Which pairs of contributions land exactly on \(k\)?

If the first die gives \(i\), then the second die is forced to give \(k-i\). That is the whole reason the formula contains the backward-looking index. More generally, if \(a_i\) is the weight of the first contribution being \(i\), and \(b_j\) is the weight of the second contribution being \(j\), then the weight at target \(k\) is \[ (a*b)_k = \sum_i a_i b_{k-i}. \tag{1}\]

Read Equation 1 as a sentence, not as a symbol:

Fix the target \(k\). Try every possible first contribution \(i\). The second contribution is then forced to be \(k-i\). Multiply the two weights. Add all the compatible stories.

That phrase, all the compatible stories, is the heart of the operation. Convolution is what happens when many little two-part explanations can lead to the same observed result, and we refuse to forget any of them.

Why one list gets flipped

Now let the stencil picture do some work. Write the first list of weights in place. Write the second list backward. Slide the reversed list past the first. At each position, multiply the overlapping entries and add.

The reversal is not a convention pasted on afterward. It is the geometry of addition. As \(i\) moves right, its complement \(k-i\) moves left. One hand walks forward while the other walks backward. The flipped strip is the formula made visible.

For fair dice, if we only count ways, both strips are \[ [1,1,1,1,1,1]. \] Slide one copy past the other. First only one pair overlaps. Then two pairs. Then three, four, five, six. Then the overlap shrinks again. The pyramid was not a probability miracle. It was the length of the overlap window.

There is one small indexing detail worth saying out loud. In the code below the list entry at index \(0\) stands for die face \(1\), index \(1\) stands for face \(2\), and so on. So output index \(r\) corresponds to the real-world total \(r+2\). That offset is not mathematics; it is just Python being zero-indexed.

def convolve(a, b):
    out = [0] * (len(a) + len(b) - 1)
    for i, ai in enumerate(a):
        for j, bj in enumerate(b):
            out[i + j] += ai * bj
    return out

die = [1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1]
counts = convolve(die, die)

print(counts)
print(sum(counts))
[1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1]
36

The output has total mass \(36\), one count for each ordered pair of die rolls. If we divide by \(36\), those counts become probabilities.

probabilities = [c / 36 for c in counts]
for total, p in zip(range(2, 13), probabilities):
    print(f"{total:2d}: {p:.3f}")

Nothing in that code knows about dice. It knows only one rule: combine all pairs whose indices add to the same target. The dice merely gave the rule a friendly face.

Loaded dice, same stencil

Let us make the dice unfair, because unfairness reveals what the multiplication in Equation 1 is doing.

Suppose the first die prefers low numbers and the second prefers high numbers. Then a total can become likely for two different reasons: many pairs may hit it, or a few especially heavy pairs may hit it. Convolution does not care which story wins. It adds the weights of all compatible stories.

low_die = [6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1]
high_die = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]

total_weight = sum(low_die) * sum(high_die)
weighted = convolve(low_die, high_die)

for total, weight in zip(range(2, 13), weighted):
    print(f"{total:2d}: {weight / total_weight:.3f}")
 2: 0.014
 3: 0.039
 4: 0.073
 5: 0.113
 6: 0.159
 7: 0.206
 8: 0.159
 9: 0.113
10: 0.073
11: 0.039
12: 0.014

This is why convolution is the natural operation for adding independent random variables. The word “independent” is exactly what lets the weight of a pair factor as \[ P(X=i,\ Y=j)=P(X=i)P(Y=j). \] Once that multiplication is legal, summing over all pairs with \(i+j=k\) gives the distribution of \(X+Y\).

So convolution is not an arbitrary new operation. It is what ordinary addition of uncertain quantities looks like when you keep every possible way the target could have happened.

Take a moment with that. The operation did not begin life as a formula. It began as a demand for honesty: if several hidden pairings can produce the same visible outcome, count them all.

Polynomials were doing it all along

Now put probability aside and multiply two polynomials: \[ (a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2)(b_0+b_1x+b_2x^2). \]

Where does the coefficient of \(x^3\) come from? It comes from every pair of terms whose exponents add to \(3\): \[ a_1b_2x^3 + a_2b_1x^3. \] More generally, the coefficient of \(x^k\) in \[ A(x)B(x) \] is \[ \sum_i a_i b_{k-i}. \]

That is the same formula again.

This is one of those moments where mathematics earns Poincare’s line in the epigraph. Adding random variables and multiplying polynomials look unrelated at the surface. One talks about probability; the other talks about algebra. But underneath, both ask for the same bookkeeping:

Which pairs of indices add to this target?

That is why probability generating functions work for nonnegative integer-valued random variables. If \[ A(x)=\sum_i P(X=i)x^i,\qquad B(x)=\sum_j P(Y=j)x^j, \] then \[ A(x)B(x) \] is the generating function for \(X+Y\). Polynomial multiplication is doing the probability convolution for you.

def poly_mul(a, b):
    return convolve(a, b)

# (1 + 2x + 3x^2)(4 + 5x + 6x^2)
A = [1, 2, 3]
B = [4, 5, 6]

print(poly_mul(A, B))  # coefficients of 4 + 13x + 28x^2 + 27x^3 + 18x^4
[4, 13, 28, 27, 18]

It is worth pausing here. A single operation has now worn two costumes:

  • in probability, it adds independent quantities;
  • in algebra, it multiplies polynomials.

The shared mechanism is not a metaphor. It is the same sum.

From lists to functions

So far our objects have been finite lists, tidy little strips with only a few slots. But the same picture survives when the strips become continuous.

Imagine two functions \(f\) and \(g\) on the real line. We want a new function whose value at \(t\) gathers every way to split \(t\) into two pieces: \[ t = \tau + (t-\tau). \] The first function pays weight \(f(\tau)\) for the first piece. The second, flipped-and-slid function pays weight \(g(t-\tau)\) for the complement. Multiply those weights and add over every possible split. Since the splits are continuous, “add” becomes “integrate”: \[ (f*g)(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(\tau)g(t-\tau)\,d\tau. \tag{2}\]

That is continuous convolution, at least for functions nice enough that the integral actually converges, say integrable functions or the well-behaved signals one usually meets first. The technical hypotheses matter in analysis, but the picture has not changed: slide, multiply overlap, add.

Again, do not let the integral intimidate you. It says the same thing Equation 1 said:

Fix the target. Try every first contribution. The second contribution is forced. Multiply. Add.

Echoes and memory

If the functions live in time and nothing happens before \(t=0\), the natural version is the causal convolution \[ (f*g)(t)=\int_0^t f(\tau)g(t-\tau)\,d\tau. \tag{3}\]

This is where convolution stops being only an overlap machine and becomes a memory machine.

Imagine shouting in a canyon. Your voice does not vanish at the instant you make it; the canyon keeps answering. A sharp clap at time \(\tau\) leaves an echo pattern after it, and by time \(t\) that pattern has aged by \(t-\tau\) seconds. If your whole input is made of many tiny claps, then the output at time \(t\) is the sum of all their aged echoes.

That is exactly Equation 3. Under the usual regularity assumptions, a linear time-invariant causal system is determined by its impulse response \(h\): the echo it gives back to one tiny unit impulse. If \(u\) is the input, the output is \[ y(t)=(h*u)(t)=\int_0^t h(t-\tau)u(\tau)\,d\tau. \tag{4}\]

The words “linear” and “time-invariant” are doing real work here. Linearity says we are allowed to add the echoes from separate impulses. Time-invariance says an impulse delayed to time \(\tau\) produces the same echo shape, just delayed. Those two promises are precisely what make the convolution formula inevitable.

This is where convolution reaches back toward the Laplace-transform story from the previous post. There, the transform exposed the exponential DNA of a function. Here, convolution says that a system with memory is not responding only to the present. It is folding the whole past into the present, one aged echo at a time.

Convolution is not a decoration on the theory. It is the arithmetic of linear memory.

Why Fourier transforms love convolution

The reason convolution becomes algorithmically powerful is that it is expensive in its native form. For two length-\(n\) lists, the direct sliding computation checks about \(n^2\) pairs. That is fine for dice and painful for long signals.

Fourier analysis changes the room in which the calculation happens.

There is a theorem, usually called the convolution theorem, which says: \[ \widehat{f*g}=\widehat f \cdot \widehat g. \tag{5}\]

Depending on the exact Fourier convention, there may be a harmless constant floating around this formula. Do not let that distract from the structural point:

In the Fourier world, convolution becomes pointwise multiplication.

This should rhyme with the Laplace post. There, differentiation became multiplication by \(s\). Here, convolution becomes multiplication frequency by frequency. In both cases, a messy operation in the original world becomes a simple operation after a transform.

That is the deeper pattern:

Original world Transform world
differentiate multiply by frequency / \(s\)
convolve multiply pointwise

Why should convolution turn into multiplication? A good intuition is that Fourier modes are the pure tones of translation. More precisely, a complex exponential \(e^{i\omega t}\), shifted in time, does not become some new species of function; it only picks up a scalar phase factor. Since convolution is built from shifted copies, the Fourier basis is exactly the coordinate system where those shifts become simple scalings. In the right coordinates, the sliding machine diagonalizes.

That last word is not accidental. If you have read the spectral theorem post, you have seen this move before. A hard linear operation becomes easy when we write it in the directions it naturally stretches. Fourier analysis is the spectral theorem for translation-shaped problems.

The computational payoff is the fast Fourier transform. Instead of doing the sliding sum directly, one can:

  1. transform both lists,
  2. multiply corresponding Fourier coefficients,
  3. transform back.

For finite lists there is one more practical wrinkle. The raw discrete Fourier transform naturally computes circular convolution, where the right edge wraps back around to the left. To recover the ordinary straight-line convolution from Equation 1, you zero-pad the lists to length at least len(a) + len(b) - 1 before transforming. With that padding in place, the direct convolution costs about \(n^2\) operations, while the FFT route costs about \(n\log n\) operations [2].

That difference is why convolution can be everywhere at industrial scale: audio filters, image blur, large integer multiplication, polynomial arithmetic, probability distributions, and the convolutional layers that gave early deep learning systems their eyes.

A checksum for the shortcut

It is useful to end with a piece of code that has no philosophy in it. We will compute the same convolution two ways: directly, by the sliding sum, and through the Fourier shortcut with zero-padding.

import numpy as np

def fft_convolve(a, b):
    n = len(a) + len(b) - 1
    A = np.fft.rfft(a, n)
    B = np.fft.rfft(b, n)
    return np.rint(np.fft.irfft(A * B, n)).astype(int).tolist()

A = [2, -1, 3]       # 2 - x + 3x^2
B = [1, 4]           # 1 + 4x

print(convolve(A, B))
print(fft_convolve(A, B))
[2, 7, -1, 12]
[2, 7, -1, 12]

The two answers agree. The first route watched every overlapping pair directly. The second route changed coordinates, multiplied frequency by frequency, and changed back. Same operation, different room. The rounding in fft_convolve is only there because the FFT travels through floating-point arithmetic; for these integer coefficients, any 2.9999999997 should plainly come home as 3.

For this tiny example the FFT route is silly. For million-point signals, or for the large polynomial multiplications that sit inside serious integer arithmetic, it is a different world.

What to remember

Convolution is the mathematics of overlap and echo.

It takes two sources of contribution and asks, for every target, how many weighted ways the two sources can combine to hit that target. That is why the same formula counts dice sums, multiplies polynomials, adds independent random variables, blurs images, filters signals, and describes the memory of a linear system.

The sliding picture is not a mnemonic pasted on afterward. It is the formula made visible: one strip reversed because complements run backward, then shifted because the target changes. The echo picture is the same idea in time: every past input leaves a delayed response, and the present is the sum of all those aged responses.

And the reason convolution keeps returning in serious mathematics is that Fourier and Laplace transforms know how to tame it. In the original world it is a sum over all overlapping histories. In the transformed world it is ordinary multiplication. A complicated memory becomes a simple product, provided you are willing to look at it in the right coordinates.

write it in the directions it naturally stretches. Fourier analysis is the
spectral theorem for translation-shaped problems.

The computational payoff is the fast Fourier transform. Instead of doing the
sliding sum directly, one can:

1. transform both lists,
2. multiply corresponding Fourier coefficients,
3. transform back.

For finite lists there is one more practical wrinkle. The raw discrete Fourier
transform naturally computes **circular** convolution, where the right edge wraps
back around to the left. To recover the ordinary straight-line convolution from
@eq-conv, you zero-pad the lists to length at least `len(a) + len(b) - 1` before
transforming. With that padding in place, the direct convolution costs about
$n^2$ operations, while the FFT route costs about $n\log n$ operations
[@oppenheim1999discrete].

That difference is why convolution can be everywhere at industrial scale: audio
filters, image blur, large integer multiplication, polynomial arithmetic,
probability distributions, and the convolutional layers that gave early deep
learning systems their eyes.

## A checksum for the shortcut

It is useful to end with a piece of code that has no philosophy in it. We will
compute the same convolution two ways: directly, by the sliding sum, and through
the Fourier shortcut with zero-padding.

```{python}
import numpy as np

def fft_convolve(a, b):
    n = len(a) + len(b) - 1
    A = np.fft.rfft(a, n)
    B = np.fft.rfft(b, n)
    return np.rint(np.fft.irfft(A * B, n)).astype(int).tolist()

A = [2, -1, 3]       # 2 - x + 3x^2
B = [1, 4]           # 1 + 4x

print(convolve(A, B))
print(fft_convolve(A, B))
```

The two answers agree. The first route watched every overlapping pair directly.
The second route changed coordinates, multiplied frequency by frequency, and
changed back. Same operation, different room. The rounding in `fft_convolve` is
only there because the FFT travels through floating-point arithmetic; for these
integer coefficients, any `2.9999999997` should plainly come home as `3`.

For this tiny example the FFT route is silly. For million-point signals, or for
the large polynomial multiplications that sit inside serious integer arithmetic,
it is a different world.

## What to remember

Convolution is the mathematics of overlap and echo.

It takes two sources of contribution and asks, for every target, how many
weighted ways the two sources can combine to hit that target. That is why the
same formula counts dice sums, multiplies polynomials, adds independent random
variables, blurs images, filters signals, and describes the memory of a linear
system.

The sliding picture is not a mnemonic pasted on afterward. It is the formula
made visible: one strip reversed because complements run backward, then shifted
because the target changes. The echo picture is the same idea in time: every
past input leaves a delayed response, and the present is the sum of all those
aged responses.

And the reason convolution keeps returning in serious mathematics is that
Fourier and Laplace transforms know how to tame it. In the original world it is
a sum over all overlapping histories. In the transformed world it is ordinary
multiplication. A complicated memory becomes a simple product, provided you are
willing to look at it in the right coordinates.
