The Spectral Theorem for Symmetric Matrices

pure
applied
linear-algebra
Why every real symmetric matrix is secretly just a stretch along perpendicular axes, told as a picture first and a proof second.
Published

June 1, 2026

“Mathematics is the art of giving the same name to different things.”

— Henri Poincaré

Here is a claim that sounds almost too clean to be true. Take any real symmetric matrix, no matter how tangled its entries look, and there is always a way to turn your head so that the transformation it describes does nothing fancy at all. It just stretches space along a set of perpendicular axes, each by its own amount. No shearing, no twisting, no mixing of directions. That is the whole content of the spectral theorem, and the rest of this post is about why space has no choice but to behave this way.

First, the picture

Picture a linear map in two dimensions. In general it can do unpleasant things: it can rotate, it can shear a square into a slanted parallelogram, it can stretch more in one diagonal direction than another. A generic matrix really does mix your coordinate axes together.

Symmetry is the condition that quietly forbids all of that mixing, once you look from the right angle. The promise of the theorem is that for a symmetric matrix you can always find a fresh set of axes, still perfectly perpendicular to one another, in which the map becomes a pure diagonal stretch. Finding those special axes is the whole game, and they are exactly the eigenvectors.

Setting it up

Let \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) be symmetric, meaning \(A^\top = A\). Write the standard inner product as \(\langle x, y\rangle = x^\top y\). The single fact that powers everything below is that symmetry lets you slide \(A\) from one side of an inner product to the other:

\[ \langle Ax, y\rangle = \langle x, Ay\rangle \qquad \text{for all } x,y \in \mathbb{R}^n. \tag{1}\]

This is worth a moment. It says \(A\) treats both slots of the inner product identically, a property called self-adjointness. Everything that follows is really Equation 1 wearing different costumes.

The theorem

Theorem 1 (Spectral Theorem) If \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) is symmetric, then there exist an orthogonal matrix \(Q\) (so \(Q^\top Q = I\)) and a real diagonal matrix \(\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)\) such that \[ A = Q \Lambda Q^\top . \tag{2}\] Equivalently, \(\mathbb{R}^n\) has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of \(A\), and every eigenvalue is real.

Read Equation 2 from right to left and it tells the same story as the picture. The matrix \(Q^\top\) rotates you into the special axes, \(\Lambda\) does the honest diagonal stretching, and \(Q\) rotates you back. We reach it through two small observations.

Two small facts

Lemma 1 (Eigenvalues are real) Every eigenvalue of a real symmetric \(A\) is real.

Proof. Let \(Ax = \lambda x\) with \(x \neq 0\), where for the moment we allow \(\lambda\) and \(x\) to be complex. Write \(x^{*}\) for the conjugate transpose, so \(\langle x, x\rangle = x^{*}x = \|x\|^2 > 0\). Because \(A\) has real entries and \(A^\top = A\), it is Hermitian: \(A^{*} = A\). Now follow a single quantity, \(x^{*}Ax\), down two different paths: \[ \lambda\, \langle x, x\rangle = x^{*} A x = x^{*} A^{*} x = (Ax)^{*} x = \bar\lambda\, \langle x, x\rangle . \] The first equality uses \(Ax = \lambda x\); the middle uses \(A^{*}=A\); the last uses \(Ax = \lambda x\) again, now inside the conjugated slot. Since \(\langle x, x\rangle > 0\) we may cancel it, leaving \(\lambda = \bar\lambda\). A number equal to its own conjugate is real. \(\qquad\blacksquare\)

Lemma 2 (Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal) If \(Ax = \lambda x\) and \(Ay = \mu y\) with \(\lambda \neq \mu\), then \(\langle x, y\rangle = 0\).

Proof. Slide \(A\) across the inner product using Equation 1: \[ \lambda \langle x, y\rangle = \langle Ax, y\rangle = \langle x, Ay\rangle = \mu \langle x, y\rangle . \] Therefore \((\lambda - \mu)\langle x, y\rangle = 0\), and since \(\lambda \neq \mu\) the only way out is \(\langle x, y\rangle = 0\). The special axes are automatically perpendicular, exactly as the picture demanded. \(\qquad\blacksquare\)

Putting it together

We now assemble the theorem. The argument is an induction on the dimension \(n\), and each step leans on the two facts above.

Proof. The base case \(n = 1\) is immediate, since a \(1\times 1\) matrix is already diagonal. For the inductive step, Lemma 1 hands us a genuine real eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\) with a unit eigenvector \(q_1\). Consider its orthogonal complement \(q_1^{\perp}\), the hyperplane of everything perpendicular to \(q_1\). Identity Equation 1 shows this hyperplane is \(A\)-invariant: if \(v \perp q_1\) then \(Av \perp q_1\) as well, so \(A\) never pushes you off the hyperplane. Restricted there, \(A\) is again symmetric but acts on a space of dimension \(n-1\), so the inductive hypothesis supplies an orthonormal eigenbasis of that smaller space. Adjoining \(q_1\) completes it to an orthonormal eigenbasis of all of \(\mathbb{R}^n\). Lining those vectors up as the columns of \(Q\) gives exactly Equation 2. \(\qquad\blacksquare\)

For a complete and careful treatment, including the version for self-adjoint operators on infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, see [1]. For the numerical side of the story, [2] is the classic reference.

A numerical check

A proof tells you a decomposition must exist; it is a different kind of satisfying to watch it appear. Let us build a random symmetric matrix, ask the computer for its eigenvalues and eigenvectors, and confirm that Equation 2 holds right down to floating-point roundoff.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 6
M = rng.standard_normal((n, n))
A = (M + M.T) / 2                      # force symmetry

eigvals, Q = np.linalg.eigh(A)         # eigh is built for symmetric matrices
Lambda = np.diag(eigvals)

reconstruction_error = np.linalg.norm(A - Q @ Lambda @ Q.T)
orthogonality_error  = np.linalg.norm(Q.T @ Q - np.eye(n))

print(f"eigenvalues (all real): {np.round(eigvals, 4)}")
print(f"|A - Q L Q^T| = {reconstruction_error:.2e}")
print(f"|Q^T Q - I|   = {orthogonality_error:.2e}")
eigenvalues (all real): [-2.0037 -0.988   0.2761  0.4913  1.5001  1.8151]
|A - Q L Q^T| = 1.62e-15
|Q^T Q - I|   = 1.39e-15

Both residuals sit around \(10^{-15}\), which is as close to zero as double-precision arithmetic ever gets. The eigenvalues come back real, the eigenvectors come back orthonormal, and the stretch-and-rotate story holds up.

What to remember

If you keep only three things from this note, keep these. Symmetry is really self-adjointness, the identity Equation 1, and that one fact is the engine of the entire proof. From it follow real eigenvalues (Lemma 1) and perpendicular eigenvectors (Lemma 2), which together build the orthonormal frame the theorem promises. And in practice, when you actually compute, reach for a symmetry-aware routine like eigh rather than the general eig; it is faster and it guarantees the real, orthogonal answer the mathematics says you deserve.

## A numerical check

A proof tells you a decomposition must exist; it is a different kind of satisfying to watch it appear. Let us build a random symmetric matrix, ask the computer for its eigenvalues and eigenvectors, and confirm that @eq-decomp holds right down to floating-point roundoff.

```{python}

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 6
M = rng.standard_normal((n, n))
A = (M + M.T) / 2                      # force symmetry

eigvals, Q = np.linalg.eigh(A)         # eigh is built for symmetric matrices

print(f"|A - Q L Q^T| = {reconstruction_error:.2e}")
print(f"|Q^T Q - I|   = {orthogonality_error:.2e}")
```

Both residuals sit around $10^{-15}$, which is as close to zero as double-precision arithmetic ever gets. The eigenvalues come back real, the eigenvectors come back orthonormal, and the stretch-and-rotate story holds up.
