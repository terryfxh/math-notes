Matrix Exponentials: When a Matrix Becomes Motion

pure
applied
linear-algebra
differential-equations
How \(e^{At}\) turns a matrix into the flow of a linear differential equation, and why growth, rotation, diffusion, probability, control, and quantum evolution all hide inside the same exponential.
Published

June 23, 2026

“Philosophy is written in this grand book, the universe … It is written in the language of mathematics, and its characters are triangles, circles, and other geometric figures.”

— Galileo Galilei, Il Saggiatore (The Assayer)

You already know one exponential so well that it has probably stopped looking strange: \[ x(t)=e^{rt}x_0. \] If \(r\) is positive, the quantity grows. If \(r\) is negative, it decays. If \(r\) is the interest rate on a bank account, \(e^{rt}\) is continuous compounding. If it is the rate constant in a chemical reaction, \(e^{rt}\) is the smooth fading of a concentration. One number \(r\) gives a rule for how strongly the present pushes itself into the future.

Now replace that number by a matrix: \[ x(t)=e^{At}x_0. \] At first this looks like notation getting too ambitious. What could it mean for a matrix to sit in the exponent of \(e\)? Is this just exponentiating each entry? Is it a trick for people who like formal power series?

No. The matrix exponential is much more concrete than that. It is the answer to one question:

If a matrix tells every point which way to start moving, what is the position of the whole space after time \(t\)?

That is the picture to keep in mind. A matrix \(A\) is not only a transformation. In this story it is an infinitesimal instruction field. At every point \(x\), the vector \(Ax\) tells that point its current velocity. The matrix exponential \(e^{At}\) is the finite motion produced by obeying those velocity instructions for \(t\) seconds.

The scalar exponential says “grow at a rate proportional to yourself.” The matrix exponential says “move at a velocity given by a linear transformation of your current position.” That single upgrade is why \(e^{At}\) shows up in differential equations, rotations, heat flow, networks, Markov chains, control systems, and quantum mechanics.

The scalar story, seen correctly

Start with the differential equation \[ x'(t)=r x(t), \qquad x(0)=x_0. \] This is the cleanest possible dynamical law: the rate of change is proportional to the current amount. Its solution is \[ x(t)=e^{rt}x_0. \]

There is a way to read this that is much more useful than “we solved an ODE.” The number \(r\) is an instantaneous multiplier. Over a tiny time step \(\Delta t\), the value approximately changes by \[ x \longmapsto x + r x\,\Delta t = (1+r\Delta t)x. \] So if we split the time interval \(t\) into \(n\) tiny pieces, each of length \(t/n\), we expect \[ x(t)\approx \left(1+\frac{rt}{n}\right)^n x_0. \] Let the time steps become infinitely fine: \[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{rt}{n}\right)^n x_0=e^{rt}x_0. \]

That limit is the heart of the exponential. It is not merely a function with a nice derivative. It is what happens when you compound infinitely many tiny “change in proportion to where you are” instructions.

Now make the same sentence vector-valued.

A matrix as an infinitesimal motion

Let \(x(t)\) be a vector, and let \(A\) be a square matrix. The linear differential equation \[ x'(t)=Ax(t), \qquad x(0)=x_0 \tag{1}\] means:

At the current point \(x\), the velocity is \(Ax\).

In the plane, \(A\) assigns a little velocity arrow to every point. Points do not all move with the same velocity. A point twice as far in a direction has twice the velocity contribution from that direction, because the rule is linear.

For a tiny time step \(\Delta t\), equation Equation 1 says \[ x(t+\Delta t)\approx x(t)+\Delta t\,Ax(t)=(I+\Delta t\,A)x(t). \] So one tiny step is almost the linear transformation \(I+\Delta t\,A\): do almost nothing, plus a small shove prescribed by \(A\).

Repeat this \(n\) times over total time \(t\): \[ x(t)\approx \left(I+\frac{tA}{n}\right)^n x_0. \] Then take the limit: \[ \boxed{\,e^{At}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(I+\frac{tA}{n}\right)^n\,} \tag{2}\] and therefore \[ x(t)=e^{At}x_0. \]

This is the most physical definition of the matrix exponential. It says:

\(e^{At}\) is the result of compounding infinitely many infinitesimal linear nudges.

The usual power series gives the same object: \[ e^{At} =I+tA+\frac{t^2A^2}{2!}+\frac{t^3A^3}{3!}+\cdots. \tag{3}\] This formula is not entrywise exponentiation. It is matrix multiplication repeated inside a series. The square matrix condition matters because the powers \(A^2,A^3,\ldots\) have to make sense. A nonsquare matrix maps between different spaces; it cannot be fed back into itself in this way without extra structure.

There is a beautiful interpretation hiding in Equation 3. The term \(A\) records direct influence. The term \(A^2\) records influence after two handoffs. The term \(A^3\) records influence after three handoffs. The exponential adds influence paths of every possible length, weighted by the time factor \(t^k/k!\). If \(A\) is a network of rates, \(e^{At}\) is not just one step through the network. It is the sum of all possible continuous-time journeys through it.

That is the first mental picture worth keeping: a matrix exponential is infinitesimal motion accumulated into finite motion.

Why it solves the differential equation

The formal reason is mercifully short. Differentiate the series term by term: \[ \frac{d}{dt}e^{At} =A+\frac{2tA^2}{2!}+\frac{3t^2A^3}{3!}+\cdots =A\left(I+tA+\frac{t^2A^2}{2!}+\cdots\right) =A e^{At}. \] Also \(e^{A\cdot 0}=I\), because every positive power of \(0A\) vanishes. Therefore \[ x(t)=e^{At}x_0 \] satisfies \[ x'(t)=A e^{At}x_0=A x(t), \qquad x(0)=Ix_0=x_0. \]

So \(e^{At}\) is the state transition matrix for the system \(x'=Ax\). Feed it the starting state, and it returns the state after time \(t\).

That phrase “transition matrix” is worth taking literally. The matrix \(A\) gives the instantaneous rule. The matrix \(e^{At}\) gives the actual transition after waiting.

If the matrix is diagonal, nothing mixes

Suppose \[ A= \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0\\ 0 & \lambda_2 \end{pmatrix}. \] Then \[ e^{At}= \begin{pmatrix} e^{\lambda_1 t} & 0\\ 0 & e^{\lambda_2 t} \end{pmatrix}. \] No mystery is left. The first coordinate grows or decays at rate \(\lambda_1\), and the second coordinate grows or decays at rate \(\lambda_2\). The two axes do not talk to each other.

This is the scalar story twice.

More generally, if \(A\) has an eigenbasis, so that \[ A=PDP^{-1}, \] then \[ e^{At}=P e^{Dt}P^{-1}. \tag{4}\] Read Equation 4 as a three-step movie:

  1. Translate the vector into the eigenvector coordinate system with \(P^{-1}\).
  2. Let each eigen-direction grow, decay, or oscillate independently by \(e^{\lambda_i t}\).
  3. Translate back with \(P\).

This is why eigenvectors are not just a computation trick. They are the coordinate system in which a continuous linear motion stops mixing its story. Each eigenvalue becomes an exponent. If the eigenvalue has negative real part, that component fades. If it has positive real part, that component grows. If it has imaginary part, then over complex coordinates, or on the corresponding real two-dimensional invariant plane, the motion oscillates.

The matrix exponential is where eigenvalues become visible as dynamics.

Rotation from an infinitesimal turn

Here is the example that makes the notation earn its keep. Consider \[ J= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1\\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \] Multiplying by \(J\) rotates a vector by \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise. So the differential equation \[ x'(t)=Jx(t) \] says:

Your velocity is always perpendicular to your current position.

What path has that property? A circle. If your velocity always points sideways relative to the radius vector, you do not fly outward or inward. You go around.

Now compute the exponential. Since \[ J^2=-I,\qquad J^3=-J,\qquad J^4=I, \] the power series splits into even and odd powers: \[ e^{tJ} =\left(1-\frac{t^2}{2!}+\frac{t^4}{4!}-\cdots\right)I +\left(t-\frac{t^3}{3!}+\frac{t^5}{5!}-\cdots\right)J. \] Those two parenthesized series are \(\cos t\) and \(\sin t\), so \[ e^{tJ}=\cos t\,I+\sin t\,J = \begin{pmatrix} \cos t & -\sin t\\ \sin t & \cos t \end{pmatrix}. \tag{5}\]

The matrix exponential has turned an infinitesimal rotation generator into an ordinary finite rotation. The matrix \(J\) says “turn sideways right now.” The matrix \(e^{tJ}\) says “after obeying that instruction for \(t\) units of time, you have rotated by angle \(t\).”

This is the second mental picture worth keeping: some matrices are not best understood as one-time transformations, but as generators of motion. The exponential is how the generator becomes the motion.

Heat between two rooms

Now take a less elegant and more physical system: two rooms exchanging heat through a wall. Let \(x_1(t)\) and \(x_2(t)\) be their temperatures, measured relative to any fixed baseline. Suppose the rooms exchange heat with each other at rate \(k\), with no outside heat source or sink. A simple model is \[ \begin{pmatrix} x_1'\\ x_2' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -k & k\\ k & -k \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x_1\\ x_2 \end{pmatrix}. \tag{6}\]

The diagonal entries \(-k\) are the self-terms: a hotter room tends to cool relative to the other. The off-diagonal entries \(k\) are the coupling terms: the other room feeds heat into it. The columns and rows are not decoration; they are little pipes of influence.

Call the matrix in Equation 6 \(A\). Two directions matter: \[ \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}. \] The first direction means “both rooms have the same temperature.” Applying \(A\) to it gives zero: \[ A\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}=0. \] No temperature difference means no heat flow. This component stays fixed.

The second direction means “one room is hot by the same amount the other is cold.” Applying \(A\) gives \[ A\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix} =-2k\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}. \] This component decays at rate \(2k\).

So if the starting temperatures are \(x_1(0)=a\) and \(x_2(0)=b\), the exponential gives \[ x_1(t)=\frac{a+b}{2}+\frac{a-b}{2}e^{-2kt}, \qquad x_2(t)=\frac{a+b}{2}-\frac{a-b}{2}e^{-2kt}. \tag{7}\]

The average temperature stays. The difference dies. That is the entire physical story, and it dropped out because \(e^{At}\) lets the eigenvectors speak in time.

This example also gives a good way to read off-diagonal entries. They are not “messy non-diagonal terms.” They are coupling. They are the places where one coordinate starts influencing another.

Volume, trace, and why flows do not suddenly collapse

For a real square matrix, there is a compact fact that deserves more visual attention than it usually gets: \[ \det(e^{At})=e^{t\,\operatorname{tr}(A)}. \tag{8}\]

The left side is the volume-scaling factor of the finite motion \(e^{At}\). The right side says that the trace of \(A\) is the instantaneous rate of volume growth.

This is easy to believe for a diagonal matrix. If \[ A=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n), \] then \[ e^{At}=\operatorname{diag}(e^{\lambda_1t},\ldots,e^{\lambda_nt}), \] and the volume scale is \[ e^{\lambda_1t}\cdots e^{\lambda_nt} =e^{t(\lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_n)} =e^{t\,\operatorname{tr}(A)}. \] The general case is the same truth written without a diagonal coordinate system.

There are two lovely consequences.

First, if \(\operatorname{tr}(A)<0\), volumes shrink exponentially. If \(\operatorname{tr}(A)>0\), volumes expand exponentially. If \(\operatorname{tr}(A)=0\), the flow preserves volume. Rotation has trace zero, and indeed a rotation does not change area.

Second, \(e^{t\,\operatorname{tr}(A)}\) is never zero. So \(e^{At}\) is always invertible, with inverse \[ (e^{At})^{-1}=e^{-At}. \] A continuous linear flow can squeeze space very hard, but it cannot flatten a plane into a line in finite time. It cannot suddenly lose a dimension. Collapse belongs to projections and singular transformations, not to finite-time exponentials of matrices.

That is a quiet but important distinction. A matrix can crush space. A matrix exponential is the motion produced by a matrix acting as a velocity law, and that motion always remains reversible for finite time.

The trap: matrices do not commute

It is tempting to carry every scalar exponential identity into matrix land: \[ e^{a+b}=e^a e^b. \] For matrices, this is generally false: \[ e^{A+B}\ne e^A e^B. \]

The reason is the oldest reason in linear algebra: order matters. A shear followed by a rotation is usually not the same as a rotation followed by a shear. Since matrix multiplication remembers order, exponentials of matrices must remember it too.

There is one clean exception. If \[ AB=BA, \] then \[ e^{A+B}=e^A e^B. \] Commuting matrices behave enough like numbers for the familiar law to return.

This warning becomes especially important when the matrix itself changes with time. If \[ x'(t)=A(t)x(t), \] you might hope the solution is simply \[ \exp\left(\int_0^t A(s)\,ds\right)x_0. \] That is only safe when the matrices \(A(s)\) commute with each other at different times. Otherwise, “what happened first” changes the answer. The true solution has to remember time ordering.

The moral is not that matrix exponentials are fragile. The moral is that they are honest. They know that geometry has order.

Where \(e^{At}\) appears

Once you recognize \(e^{At}\) as “the finite motion generated by the linear rule \(A\),” its applications stop looking like separate tricks.

In linear differential equations, it is the fundamental solution of \(x'=Ax\). The scalar solution \(e^{rt}\) was only the one-dimensional case.

In control theory, a system with input \[ x'(t)=Ax(t)+Bu(t) \] has solution \[ x(t)=e^{At}x_0+\int_0^t e^{A(t-s)}Bu(s)\,ds. \] The first term is what the system does on its own. The integral adds the effect of every past input, transported forward by the same transition matrix.

In continuous-time Markov chains, the generator matrix \(Q\) contains jump rates between states. Under the column-vector convention, its off-diagonal entries are nonnegative and each column sums to zero. If \(p(t)\) is the probability vector, then \[ p'(t)=Qp(t), \qquad p(t)=e^{tQ}p(0). \] The exponential turns instantaneous jump rates into finite-time transition probabilities.

In diffusion and network flow, matrices like graph Laplacians move quantity from a node to its neighbors. The exponential \(e^{tA}\) describes how heat, mass, or information spreads after time \(t\). The powers \(A^k\) in the series are the algebraic shadows of paths through the network.

In quantum mechanics, Schrödinger’s equation has the form \[ i\hbar\,\psi'(t)=H\psi(t), \] so \[ \psi(t)=e^{-itH/\hbar}\psi(0). \] When \(H\) is Hermitian, this exponential is unitary, which means the evolution preserves total probability. Again, the exponential turns an infinitesimal law into finite motion.

Even numerical computation fits the same theme. In practice, large matrix exponentials are not usually computed by naively summing Equation 3 term by term. Algorithms exploit scaling, squaring, Krylov subspaces, diagonalization when available, or the action \(e^{At}v\) directly. The object is defined by the series, but the most efficient way to use it depends on the geometry and size of the problem.

What to remember

The matrix exponential is not a decorative analogy with the scalar exponential. It is the finite flow generated by the linear velocity law \[ x'=Ax. \] The matrix \(A\) tells each point how to start moving. The matrix \(e^{At}\) tells where every point lands after time \(t\).

When \(A\) has an eigenbasis, those eigenvectors are the directions in which the motion becomes simple: each one grows, decays, or oscillates according to \(e^{\lambda t}\). If \(A\) couples coordinates, the powers \(A^2,A^3,\ldots\) encode longer chains of influence. If \(A\) generates rotation, its exponential is a rotation matrix. If \(A\) generates diffusion, its exponential spreads heat. If \(A\) generates a Markov chain, its exponential gives transition probabilities.

So the slogan is:

A matrix is an instantaneous rule. Its exponential is that rule lived through time.

That is why \(e^{At}\) belongs next to the determinant, eigenvectors, and change of basis in the mental toolbox of linear algebra. It is where matrices stop being static transformations and start becoming dynamics.

$$
\frac{d}{dt}e^{At}
=A+\frac{2tA^2}{2!}+\frac{3t^2A^3}{3!}+\cdots
=A\left(I+tA+\frac{t^2A^2}{2!}+\cdots\right)
=A e^{At}.
$$
Also $e^{A\cdot 0}=I$, because every positive power of $0A$ vanishes. Therefore
$$
x(t)=e^{At}x_0
$$
satisfies
$$
x'(t)=A e^{At}x_0=A x(t),
\qquad
x(0)=Ix_0=x_0.
$$

So $e^{At}$ is the **state transition matrix** for the system $x'=Ax$. Feed it
the starting state, and it returns the state after time $t$.

That phrase "transition matrix" is worth taking literally. The matrix $A$ gives
the instantaneous rule. The matrix $e^{At}$ gives the actual transition after
waiting.

## Rotation from an infinitesimal turn

Here is the example that makes the notation earn its keep. Consider
$$
J=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & -1\\
1 & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Multiplying by $J$ rotates a vector by $90^\circ$ counterclockwise. So the
differential equation
$$
x'(t)=Jx(t)
$$
says:

> Your velocity is always perpendicular to your current position.

What path has that property? A circle. If your velocity always points sideways
relative to the radius vector, you do not fly outward or inward. You go around.

Now compute the exponential. Since
$$
J^2=-I,\qquad J^3=-J,\qquad J^4=I,
$$
the power series splits into even and odd powers:
$$
e^{tJ}
=\left(1-\frac{t^2}{2!}+\frac{t^4}{4!}-\cdots\right)I
+\left(t-\frac{t^3}{3!}+\frac{t^5}{5!}-\cdots\right)J.
$$
Those two parenthesized series are $\cos t$ and $\sin t$, so
$$
e^{tJ}=\cos t\,I+\sin t\,J
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos t & -\sin t\\
\sin t & \cos t
\end{pmatrix}.
$$ {#eq-rotation}

The matrix exponential has turned an infinitesimal rotation generator into an
ordinary finite rotation. The matrix $J$ says "turn sideways right now." The
matrix $e^{tJ}$ says "after obeying that instruction for $t$ units of time, you
have rotated by angle $t$."

This is the second mental picture worth keeping: **some matrices are not best
understood as one-time transformations, but as generators of motion.** The
exponential is how the generator becomes the motion.

## Heat between two rooms

Now take a less elegant and more physical system: two rooms exchanging heat
through a wall. Let $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ be their temperatures, measured
relative to any fixed baseline. Suppose the rooms exchange heat with each other
at rate $k$, with no outside heat source or sink. A simple model is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
x_1'\\
x_2'
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
-k & k\\
k & -k
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
x_1\\
x_2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$ {#eq-two-room}

The diagonal entries $-k$ are the self-terms: a hotter room tends to cool
relative to the other. The off-diagonal entries $k$ are the coupling terms: the
other room feeds heat into it. The columns and rows are not decoration; they are
little pipes of influence.

Call the matrix in @eq-two-room $A$. Two directions matter:
$$
\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The first direction means "both rooms have the same temperature." Applying $A$
to it gives zero:
$$
A\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}=0.
$$
No temperature difference means no heat flow. This component stays fixed.

The second direction means "one room is hot by the same amount the other is
cold." Applying $A$ gives
$$
A\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}
=-2k\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
This component decays at rate $2k$.

So if the starting temperatures are $x_1(0)=a$ and $x_2(0)=b$, the exponential
gives
$$
x_1(t)=\frac{a+b}{2}+\frac{a-b}{2}e^{-2kt},
\qquad
x_2(t)=\frac{a+b}{2}-\frac{a-b}{2}e^{-2kt}.
$$ {#eq-two-room-solution}

The average temperature stays. The difference dies. That is the entire physical
story, and it dropped out because $e^{At}$ lets the eigenvectors speak in time.

This example also gives a good way to read off-diagonal entries. They are not
"messy non-diagonal terms." They are coupling. They are the places where one
coordinate starts influencing another.

## Volume, trace, and why flows do not suddenly collapse

For a real square matrix, there is a compact fact that deserves more visual
attention than it usually gets:
$$
\det(e^{At})=e^{t\,\operatorname{tr}(A)}.
$$ {#eq-det-trace}

The left side is the volume-scaling factor of the finite motion $e^{At}$. The
right side says that the trace of $A$ is the **instantaneous rate of volume
growth**.

This is easy to believe for a diagonal matrix. If
$$
A=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n),
$$
then
$$
e^{At}=\operatorname{diag}(e^{\lambda_1t},\ldots,e^{\lambda_nt}),
$$
and the volume scale is
$$
e^{\lambda_1t}\cdots e^{\lambda_nt}
=e^{t(\lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_n)}
=e^{t\,\operatorname{tr}(A)}.
$$
The general case is the same truth written without a diagonal coordinate
system.

There are two lovely consequences.

First, if $\operatorname{tr}(A)<0$, volumes shrink exponentially. If
$\operatorname{tr}(A)>0$, volumes expand exponentially. If
$\operatorname{tr}(A)=0$, the flow preserves volume. Rotation has trace zero,
and indeed a rotation does not change area.

Second, $e^{t\,\operatorname{tr}(A)}$ is never zero. So $e^{At}$ is always
invertible, with inverse
$$
(e^{At})^{-1}=e^{-At}.
$$
A continuous linear flow can squeeze space very hard, but it cannot flatten a
plane into a line in finite time. It cannot suddenly lose a dimension. Collapse
belongs to projections and singular transformations, not to finite-time
exponentials of matrices.

That is a quiet but important distinction. A matrix can crush space. A matrix
exponential is the motion produced by a matrix acting as a velocity law, and
that motion always remains reversible for finite time.

## The trap: matrices do not commute

It is tempting to carry every scalar exponential identity into matrix land:
$$
e^{a+b}=e^a e^b.
$$
For matrices, this is generally false:
$$
e^{A+B}\ne e^A e^B.
$$

The reason is the oldest reason in linear algebra: order matters. A shear
followed by a rotation is usually not the same as a rotation followed by a
shear. Since matrix multiplication remembers order, exponentials of matrices
must remember it too.

There is one clean exception. If
$$
AB=BA,
$$
then
$$
e^{A+B}=e^A e^B.
$$
Commuting matrices behave enough like numbers for the familiar law to return.

This warning becomes especially important when the matrix itself changes with
time. If
$$
x'(t)=A(t)x(t),
$$
you might hope the solution is simply
$$
\exp\left(\int_0^t A(s)\,ds\right)x_0.
$$
That is only safe when the matrices $A(s)$ commute with each other at different
times. Otherwise, "what happened first" changes the answer. The true solution
has to remember time ordering.

The moral is not that matrix exponentials are fragile. The moral is that they
are honest. They know that geometry has order.

## Where $e^{At}$ appears

Once you recognize $e^{At}$ as "the finite motion generated by the linear rule
$A$," its applications stop looking like separate tricks.

In **linear differential equations**, it is the fundamental solution of
$x'=Ax$. The scalar solution $e^{rt}$ was only the one-dimensional case.

In **control theory**, a system with input
$$
x'(t)=Ax(t)+Bu(t)
$$
has solution
$$
x(t)=e^{At}x_0+\int_0^t e^{A(t-s)}Bu(s)\,ds.
$$
The first term is what the system does on its own. The integral adds the effect
of every past input, transported forward by the same transition matrix.

In **continuous-time Markov chains**, the generator matrix $Q$ contains jump
rates between states. Under the column-vector convention, its off-diagonal
entries are nonnegative and each column sums to zero. If $p(t)$ is the
probability vector, then
$$
p'(t)=Qp(t),
\qquad
p(t)=e^{tQ}p(0).
$$
The exponential turns instantaneous jump rates into finite-time transition
probabilities.

In **diffusion and network flow**, matrices like graph Laplacians move quantity
from a node to its neighbors. The exponential $e^{tA}$ describes how heat,
mass, or information spreads after time $t$. The powers $A^k$ in the series are
the algebraic shadows of paths through the network.

In **quantum mechanics**, Schrödinger's equation has the form
$$
i\hbar\,\psi'(t)=H\psi(t),
$$
so
$$
\psi(t)=e^{-itH/\hbar}\psi(0).
$$
When $H$ is Hermitian, this exponential is unitary, which means the evolution
preserves total probability. Again, the exponential turns an infinitesimal law
into finite motion.

Even numerical computation fits the same theme. In practice, large matrix
exponentials are not usually computed by naively summing @eq-series term by
term. Algorithms exploit scaling, squaring, Krylov subspaces, diagonalization
when available, or the action $e^{At}v$ directly. The object is defined by the
series, but the most efficient way to use it depends on the geometry and size of
the problem.

## What to remember

The matrix exponential is not a decorative analogy with the scalar exponential.
It is the finite flow generated by the linear velocity law
$$
x'=Ax.
$$
The matrix $A$ tells each point how to start moving. The matrix $e^{At}$ tells
where every point lands after time $t$.

When $A$ has an eigenbasis, those eigenvectors are the directions in which the
motion becomes simple: each one grows, decays, or oscillates according to
$e^{\lambda t}$. If $A$ couples coordinates, the powers $A^2,A^3,\ldots$ encode
longer chains of influence. If $A$ generates rotation, its exponential is a
rotation matrix. If $A$ generates diffusion, its exponential spreads heat. If
$A$ generates a Markov chain, its exponential gives transition probabilities.

So the slogan is:

> A matrix is an instantaneous rule. Its exponential is that rule lived through
> time.

That is why $e^{At}$ belongs next to the determinant, eigenvectors, and change
of basis in the mental toolbox of linear algebra. It is where matrices stop
being static transformations and start becoming dynamics.
