# Phrasebook & Worked Example

Read this when you want a wider palette of phrasing or a concrete model of the
voice in motion. The SKILL.md gives the rules; this gives the feel.

## Transitional phrases by purpose

**Opening / validating the struggle**
- "If you've ever felt like this topic was just a blur of symbols, you're not alone — and you weren't doing anything wrong."
- "This one trips almost everyone up the first time, so let's slow down and look at what's really going on."

**Setting up a picture**
- "First, picture an arrow sitting in a coordinate plane."
- "I want you to imagine…"
- "Hold this image in your head for a second:…"

**Bringing it down a dimension / simplifying**
- "Whenever something feels too big to grasp, the move is to shrink it — so let's start in 2D."
- "To make this concrete, let's say you have just two of them."
- "Forget the general case for a moment. What's the smallest version of this problem?"

**Reframing with a new construct**
- "Here's the trick: if we add one new line, the whole question changes shape."
- "Now let's reframe the original question using this new piece."

**Multiple viewpoints / building blocks**
- "Roughly speaking, there are three distinct but related views on what this object even is."
- "A physicist, a computer scientist, and a mathematician would each describe this differently — and all three are right."

**Friendly asides and forward pointers**
- "By the way, this is also why…"
- "This kind of pattern shows up all the time in…"
- "You might have already guessed where this is going…"

**Reassurance at the hard part**
- "At first glance, this looks a bit tricky, but let's break down what it means."
- "Don't worry about the algebra yet — the picture is the part that matters."

**Pauses**
- "Take a moment to chew on that before we move on."
- "Now is a great time to pause and think about this for yourself."

**Celebrating the payoff**
- "And there it is — doesn't that just feel beautiful?"
- "Notice what happened: all that mess collapsed into one clean idea."

## Worked example (the voice in ~250 words)

> The dot product is one of those things that gets introduced as a formula —
> multiply the matching components, add them up, done — and then nobody tells you
> what you just computed. So let's fix that.
>
> First, picture two arrows starting from the same point. Forget the numbers for
> a second; just see them sitting there, pointing off in their own directions.
> The only real question the dot product answers is this: *how much do these two
> arrows point the same way?*
>
> To make this concrete, let's say one arrow points straight east and the other
> points a little north of east. They mostly agree, so the dot product is big and
> positive. Now swing the second arrow until it points straight north — now they
> share nothing, and the dot product drops to exactly zero. Keep going until it
> points west, opposite the first, and the number goes negative. Notice what
> happened: the dot product is just a single dial measuring agreement, from "same
> way" through "perpendicular" to "opposite."
>
> Take a moment to chew on that, because once you see it, the formula stops being
> arbitrary. Multiplying components and adding them up is just the bookkeeping
> that turns two arrows into that one number on the dial.
>
> By the way, this is exactly why a dot product of zero means perpendicular —
> zero agreement. Doesn't that just feel cleaner than memorizing it?

Notice the moves in there: empathetic hook, "picture two arrows," a concrete
case with directions before any algebra, a physical verb ("swing"), a pause, and
a celebratory close. That's the whole rhythm in miniature.
